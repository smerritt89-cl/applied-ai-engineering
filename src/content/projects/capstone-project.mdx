## The brief

Each studio project proved one skill in isolation: verified extraction, cited retrieval, a calibrated eval harness, a guarded agent, a fine-tuned or open model, a production service. The capstone asks a harder question: **can you put them together for a real user, and prove the result is good enough to use?**

You will build one system that combines retrieval, an agent with tools, and an eval suite, then harden it for production and defend it. The defense is the part most portfolio projects skip. A reviewer (a hiring manager, a staff engineer, a client) will not ask "does it run?" They will ask how you know it works, compared with what, how sure you are, what it costs, and what happens when someone attacks it. Your write-up and demo should answer those questions before they are asked.

The deliverable is not the most impressive demo. It is the most **convincing evidence**: a modest system with a sealed held-out set, a calibrated judge, and honest intervals beats an ambitious one that only works on the examples in the video.

## What makes a strong capstone

**A real user with a real job.** Pick someone whose work you understand well enough to tell a good output from a plausible one. Your PM and education-services background is an advantage here: course-development teams, instructional designers, proposal writers, and delivery leads all have repetitive, document-heavy work where retrieval and agents can help and where errors have visible costs. The strongest capstones start from a task someone does today, by hand, and can say how long it takes and how often it goes wrong.

**No confidential data, anywhere.** The repo and write-up are public. Use public corpora (open courseware, published standards such as WCAG, public RFPs from government procurement portals, open-licensed style guides), or build a **synthetic** corpus that mirrors the shape of real work without any real client, learner, contract, or employee information. If you build on your own work experience, describe the pattern, never the client. When in doubt, leave it out, and say in the write-up how the data was made.

**A problem that rewards measurement.** Good capstone problems have answers that can be checked: a requirement is either in the RFP or not, a citation supports the claim or doesn't, a task was created with the right owner or wasn't. If you can't imagine writing 100 test cases with expected outcomes, the problem is too vague for six weeks.

**A baseline worth beating.** Google's Rules of Machine Learning open with the advice not to be afraid of launching without machine learning, and to design metrics first. Apply both: measure the simplest credible alternative (the manual process, keyword search, or one model call with no retrieval or tools) on your eval set in week 1. If the baseline is already good enough, you have learned something valuable cheaply, and you should pick a harder slice of the problem.

## Scope guidance

Six weeks at about 12 hours a week is roughly 70 hours. That is enough for **one user, one core job, two to five tools, and a corpus of a few hundred to a few thousand documents**. It is not enough for a platform, a multi-tenant product, or three user types.

- **Narrow the job, not the rigor.** If you are behind, cut features, never the held-out split, the judge calibration, or the injection suite.
- **Prefer a workflow where it works.** Anthropic's guidance on building effective agents recommends the simplest solution that works and adding agentic autonomy only when it pays for itself. Your agent should earn its loop: if a fixed sequence of calls does the job, build that, and use the agent only for the part that needs judgment about which tool to call next.
- **Write non-goals down.** Ubl's account of design docs at Google treats non-goals (things that could reasonably be goals but you are deliberately excluding) as a core section. They are your defense against scope creep.
- **Budget the evaluation.** Plan for evals, iteration, and write-up to take at least 40 percent of your hours. Week 4 and week 6 are not slack.

## Example projects at the right size

Each of these is sized for six weeks and uses only public or synthetic data. Treat them as starting points; your own idea is better if it meets the criteria above.

1. **Accessibility reviewer for course pages.** Retrieval over WCAG 2.2 success criteria and an open-licensed style guide. The agent fetches an HTML course page, runs deterministic checks (alt text, heading order, link text, contrast where computable), and asks the model only for what code can't judge, citing the success criterion for each finding. Eval: 100 or more pages or fragments with expert-labeled findings. Success metric: findings an instructional designer agrees with, per page, versus a linter-only baseline.
2. **RFP compliance matrix builder.** Over public RFPs from government procurement portals, extract every requirement with a verbatim evidence quote, classify it (mandatory, desirable, informational), and map it to a synthetic capability library through a search tool, flagging gaps. Eval: requirements labeled by hand on a set of RFPs. Baseline: a single long-context call with no retrieval or verification.
3. **Outcome alignment checker for a syllabus.** Over open courseware (for example MIT OpenCourseWare or OpenStax materials, respecting their licenses), retrieve a course's stated outcomes and its assessments, and judge whether each assessment measures an outcome, with the evidence. The judge is the product here, so calibration against your own labels carries the project.
4. **Course-build status assistant.** A synthetic project tracker (tasks, owners, due dates, dependencies) for a fictional course-development program, exposed through an MCP server with read tools and one write tool behind confirmation. The agent answers "what is late and why" questions, computes slippage in code, and drafts (never sends) a status note. Eval: questions with computed ground truth from the synthetic data, plus should-abstain questions.
5. **Meeting-to-tracker agent.** Extends your Studio 1 project: public meeting transcripts (for example, published city council or board meetings) or synthetic ones, extraction with verified evidence, owner resolution through a directory tool, and task creation in a sandbox tracker through MCP, with every write confirmed. Eval: labeled commitments, plus injection cases hidden in transcripts.

<Callout type="tip" title="The one-sentence test">
Before week 1 ends, finish this sentence: "For [user], who today [current process, with a number], my system [does the job], and I will know it works when [metric] beats [baseline] on held-out data by [margin]." If you can't fill every bracket, keep scoping.
</Callout>

## Likely reviewer questions

Write a short answer to each in your repo (`DEFENSE.md`) before you record the demo. If an answer is "I don't know," that is a finding: say so, and say how you would find out.

1. Who is the user, and what did they do before your system existed? How do you know?
2. What is the baseline, and by how much does the final system beat it on held-out data, with what interval?
3. Why an agent rather than a fixed workflow? What would break if you removed the loop?
4. How was the eval set built, by whom, and how do you know it resembles real use? How many variants did you try against dev?
5. How well does your judge agree with human labels, and how did you correct for its errors?
6. What is the smallest improvement your eval set can detect?
7. What happens when a retrieved document contains instructions? Show the test.
8. Which tool could cause the most damage if misused, and what stops it?
9. What does one task cost, what is p95 latency, and what would it cost at a realistic monthly volume?
10. Where does it fail, and what would you tell a user not to use it for?
11. If you had two more weeks, what would you change first, and why that?
12. What did AI assistants write, and how did you check it?

## Pitfalls

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Scope creep">
The usual capstone failure is not a bad system but an unfinished one: week 5 arrives with three half-built features and no held-out run. The cause is almost always additions that felt small. Keep a non-goals list in the scope doc, and treat every addition as a trade: name what you are cutting to make room, and log the decision. If a feature isn't covered by your eval set, it doesn't exist for the defense.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Demo-ware without evals">
A polished demo on five examples you chose proves that five examples work. Reviewers who have shipped AI systems know this and will discount it. Build the eval set in week 1, before the system, so the examples you demo are drawn from it rather than the other way round. Show at least one failure or abstention in the demo, and point to the eval table for how often that happens.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="No baseline">
"82 percent correct" means nothing without a comparison. If a single model call with no retrieval gets 78 percent, your retrieval and agent bought 4 points (and maybe an interval that includes zero) at several times the cost. Measure the baseline first, on the same cases, with the same graders, and report every result as a paired difference against it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Tuning on the held-out set">
Every time you look at held-out results and then change something, the held-out set becomes a little more like dev. Run it at milestones only (end of weeks 2, 4, and 6), log each run, and report the count in your write-up.
</Callout>

## Constraints

- Public repo. No confidential, client, learner, or employee data, including in commit history, test fixtures, traces, and screenshots. Synthetic data is labeled as synthetic.
- Corpus documents must be public, licensed for your use, or written by you; record the source and license of each in the ingestion manifest.
- Official SDKs for model calls; keys from environment variables locally and repository secrets in CI.
- The offline test suite (graders, statistics, verification, tool logic) runs without an API key, using recorded outputs or a fake client.
- A full judged run over dev and held-out should cost under $15 at current prices. Log it.

## Report template

Put this in `REPORT.md`, and publish it (repo README link, GitHub Pages, or a blog). Aim for four to six pages plus appendices. Google's Technical Writing One course recommends stating scope and audience and putting key points first, because readers may stop after the first paragraph; the template is built that way.

## Demo video script outline

Five to eight minutes, recorded with captions. Write the script before you record, rehearse it once, and record in a fresh environment so nothing depends on state you forgot about.

1. **The user and the problem (45 seconds).** Who, what they do today, and the number that makes it painful. No architecture yet.
2. **The result first (30 seconds).** The headline from your eval table: final versus baseline on held-out, with the interval, and cost per task.
3. **A real task, end to end (2 minutes).** One case drawn from the eval set, not a hand-picked showcase. Narrate what the agent decides and why, and show the citations being checked.
4. **A failure or abstention (1 minute).** A case where the system says it doesn't know, or gets it wrong. Say how often this happens, from the eval table.
5. **An attack (45 seconds).** One injection case from your suite (for example, instructions hidden in a retrieved document) and what stops it.
6. **Under the hood (1 to 1.5 minutes).** The architecture overview, one trace with its cost and latency, and the CI gate catching a regression.
7. **Limits and next steps (30 seconds).** What you would not use it for, and what you would build next.

The limitations section borrows from model cards (Mitchell and colleagues, 2019), which ask authors to state intended uses, uses that are out of scope, and caveats, so that readers can decide whether a system fits their situation. If you can find a peer or mentor to play reviewer, give them the reviewer questions and the rubric, and record what they asked that you hadn't anticipated.
