## The brief

Build a GPT yourself, train it on a text you chose, and find out by experiment what its size and context length actually buy you. This is the lecture's suggested exercise EX2 ("train the GPT on your own dataset of choice"), turned into a small research project with the checks a working engineer would expect.

The goal is understanding, not a good model. A character-level GPT trained for minutes on one book will produce text with the shape of its source and very little sense. What you can produce is a careful, reproducible account of how loss responds to context length, width, and depth on your data, and a clear explanation of the mechanism at the center of every modern language model, in your own words.

## What you get

- **Lab e1** (`labs/electives/e1-tiny-gpt/`): tested helpers for corpus cleaning, tokenization, splitting, batching, and softmax, plus `train_outline.py`, a training script with evaluation, CSV logging, and sampling already written, and the model left for you.
- **Lessons e1-w4-l7 and e1-w4-l8**: the model, piece by piece, and the tokenizer for the stretch goal.
- **Reference code to compare against after you write your own**: Karpathy's `ng-video-lecture` and `nanoGPT` (both MIT). nanoGPT's README reports a best validation loss of 1.4697 on tiny Shakespeare for a 6-layer, 6-head, 384-dimension, context-256 model (about 3 minutes on one A100), and about 1.88 for a 4-layer, 4-head, 128-dimension, context-64 CPU run. Different corpora have different achievable losses, so these are sanity anchors, not targets.

## Choosing your corpus

Pick something you have the right to use: a public-domain book (Project Gutenberg's permissions page notes that most of its books are public domain in the US and that status can differ elsewhere), a collection of several books by one author, or your own writing. Download Gutenberg texts by hand, since the site is intended for human users and may block automated access. One to a few megabytes is a good size for laptop training. Record where the text came from, why you may use it, and how you cleaned it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Fix the budget before you compare">
A bigger model trained for the same number of steps does more compute per step, and a longer context sees more characters per step. Decide up front what "fair" means for your comparisons (same steps, same wall-clock time, or same number of training characters seen) and state it in the write-up. Without a stated budget, "bigger was better" can't be interpreted.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Too good to be true is usually a leak">
If validation loss falls far below the published baselines for a similar configuration, or far below the training loss, suspect your causal mask or your split before you suspect a breakthrough. The causal-mask test (requirement r4) and a contiguous split exist precisely to rule these out.
</Callout>

## Constraints

- Your own model code. Read the reference implementations only after yours works, and note in the write-up anything you changed after reading them.
- Everything must run on a laptop CPU at the baseline settings in under about 15 minutes per run; a GPU or Apple Silicon is optional.
- No copyrighted corpus in a public repository. If your text can't be shared, publish a script that downloads or rebuilds it, or describe how to obtain it.

## Write-up template

Put this in `WRITEUP.md` in your project repo and fill it in. Aim for two to three pages including figures.

```markdown
# Tiny GPT on <your corpus>: write-up

## Corpus
Source, why you may use it, cleaning steps, size in characters, vocabulary size, split.

## Model
Architecture (layers, heads, embedding size, context length, dropout), parameter count,
optimizer and learning rate. What you wrote yourself; what you compared against afterwards.

## Checks
- Step-0 loss vs uniform baseline log V: <numbers>, and the explanation of any gap
- Causal-mask test and helper tests: what each verifies
- Any further check (loop-based attention comparison, gradient check)

## Experiments
Budget used for fair comparison (steps, wall-clock, or characters seen): ...

| Run | block_size | n_embd | n_layer | Params | Train loss | Val loss | Time | Hardware |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| baseline | | | | | | | | |
| ... | | | | | | | | |

Loss-curve figure(s). Run-to-run noise (if measured).

## Results and analysis
For each factor: what changed, why (underfitting vs overfitting, capacity, compute),
and one result that surprised you.

## Samples
Two or three short samples per configuration, with temperature and seed.
What differs qualitatively, and what the samples can't tell you.

## Attention, in my own words
Your explanation and a diagram (hand-drawn photo, Mermaid, or any tool).
Cover queries, keys, values, the scaled softmax as a weighted average,
the causal mask, and why positions must be embedded.

## Limitations and next steps
What this model can't do, and what you'd try next (BPE tokens, more data, pretraining then fine-tuning).

## How I used AI assistants
Which tools, for what, and what you had to correct.
```
