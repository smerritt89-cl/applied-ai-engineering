## The brief

This elective asked two questions. Which model should you use for which job, and how do you know? And how should you weigh the big public claims about AI? This project answers both for you personally, with evidence you gathered yourself.

**Part 1, the playbook.** Pick three task types from your actual work. Write down what a good output looks like before you see any model output. Run at least three models on the same inputs: a Claude model, at least one of GPT, Gemini, or Grok, and at least one cheaper tier or open-weight model. Grade fairly, report results with intervals, cost, and latency, and turn them into a playbook: for each task, which model you start with, which is the fallback, what check you apply to the output, and what would make you re-run the eval. Extend it with rows for common task types from lesson 3, clearly labeled as based on documented claims rather than your own measurements.

**Part 2, the claim brief.** Pick one public claim about AI made in 2026 by a named person or organization: a lab leader's timeline, a critic's prediction, a productivity figure, a safety or policy claim. Write a two-page, evidence-weighted brief that someone outside engineering could act on.

Build it in its own repository (`ai-playbook`). Keep it small and honest. Forty-five well-chosen inputs graded carefully are worth more than five hundred graded carelessly.

## What you get

- **Lesson 3** gives you the providers' documented strengths, the independent evidence and its caveats, the playbook table to extend, and the Wilson lower-bound selection rule.
- **Lessons 4 to 6** give you worked claim boxes to model your brief on, and lesson 9 gives you the six-part checklist and a full worked example.
- **Studio 3** gives you the eval machinery (judge calibration, paired comparisons, intervals); reuse it if you have it, but nothing here requires it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the rubric before you see an output">
The single most common way to spoil a model comparison is to look at a few outputs, decide which model you like, and then write criteria that favor it. Commit the rubric first; the git history is your proof. If you later realize the rubric missed something, revise it, say why in the write-up, and regrade every model with the new version.
</Callout>

## Pitfalls

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Comparing models under different conditions">
Different prompts, different effort settings, different dates, or a retry policy applied to one model and not another all invalidate a comparison. Fix the prompt, record the exact model id and reasoning or effort setting for each provider, run everything in the same window, and log it all. If a provider requires a different prompt format, note the difference and keep the content the same.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="A judge that grades its own family">
If an LLM grades the outputs, it must not be from a family you are comparing, and you must report how often it agrees with your own labels on at least 20 items, broken down into how often it catches failures and how often it passes good outputs. Self-preference is documented (Panickssery and colleagues, 2024). When in doubt, grade with code and by hand.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Sending data you should not">
Before you put work data into any provider's API, read its data terms for the surface you are using and your employer's policy. Free tiers and consumer apps often have different terms from paid API access. If in doubt, build realistic synthetic inputs that preserve the difficulty of the real ones, and say so in the write-up.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="A brief that argues with a headline">
Find the primary source and quote the claim with its hedges. If the only source is secondhand, say so and lower your confidence. Then rewrite the claim so it could clearly fail before you weigh any evidence.
</Callout>

## Constraints

- Use each provider's official SDK or documented HTTP API, with keys in environment variables, never in the repository.
- Keep the total model spend under about 20 dollars at current prices; the runner should print an estimated cost and ask for confirmation before a full run.
- Every number in the playbook must trace to a logged run (model id, settings, date, inputs version) or be labeled as a documented claim with a link.

## Claim brief template

Put this in `CLAIM_BRIEF.md`. Two pages at most.

```markdown
# Claim brief: <short name of the claim>

## The claim
Full quotation (including hedges), speaker, role, date, and link to the primary source.

## The claim, rewritten so it can fail
One sentence with a date or a measurable outcome.

## Who is saying it, and why
Access to evidence; incentives; track record on similar, scored claims if any.

## Evidence for
Three to five points, each with a source and date. Say for each whether it is measured or extrapolated.

## Evidence against, or important nuance
Three to five points, each with a source and date. State the strongest opposing case fairly.

## Base rates
How have similar claims fared? How often do results of this kind replicate?

## Verdict
One paragraph. Your probability that the rewritten claim resolves true, and why.

## What would change my mind
Specific, dated evidence that would move the probability up, and evidence that would move it down.

## What a decision-maker should do now
Low-regret actions, and the signals that would justify larger ones.
```

## Write-up template

Put this in `WRITEUP.md` and fill it in.

```markdown
# AI playbook: write-up

## Summary (read this first)
Three to five sentences: which model you will use for each of your three tasks, how sure you are,
what it costs, and the one result that surprised you.

## Tasks and rubrics
Each task in two or three sentences, its rubric, and a link to the commit where the rubric was added.

## The eval
- Inputs: count per task, how you sourced them, how you removed sensitive data, the awkward cases
- Models: exact ids, effort or reasoning settings, dates run
- Grading: code checks, your labels, any judge (family, agreement with your labels)

## Results
| Task | Model | Pass rate (95% interval, n) | Cost per task | Median latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Read failures: at least three examples with your categorization.

## Decisions
For each task: first choice, fallback, the output check, and the re-evaluation trigger. Which close calls
would need more data, and roughly how much.

## Data and terms
For each provider used: retention, training on your data, zero data retention availability, date checked.

## The claim brief in one paragraph
The claim, your probability, and the evidence that would change it.

## Limitations
What this eval cannot detect, what you would measure next, and when you will rerun it.

## How I used AI assistants
Which tools, for what, and what you had to check or correct.
```

When you finish, score yourself with the rubric below (ideally a day later), paste your repo link, and mark the project submitted. Then put a reminder in your calendar for three months from now to rerun the eval on whatever has been released since.
