## Valid JSON, invented facts

By the end of lesson 8 your extractor can't produce malformed output. Now look at what it can still produce. Picture a transcript where Sam says "I can update the roadmap slide, but I need the final numbers first." A schema-valid extraction might read: task "Update the roadmap slide", owner "Sam", due date 2026-11-14. The owner is plausible. The due date appears nowhere. Nothing in the JSON looks different from a correct item, and the date is formatted perfectly.

This is the central trust problem of extraction. The schema guarantees shape; it doesn't make values true. Google's Gemini docs warn about schema-compliant but semantically incorrect outputs, and Anthropic's own guidance on hallucinations says that even with good techniques you should validate critical information. Constrained decoding can also add pressure to invent: if a field is required and non-nullable, the model must write something.

The studio project grades you on this directly. A list of action items is easy to produce. A list where every item can be traced to what was actually said, with doubtful items flagged instead of silently kept, is the deliverable. This lesson builds the checks, from cheap and certain to expensive and fallible, and ends with how to decide what a person should look at.
</Listen>

## Require evidence, then verify it in code

The single most useful check for extraction is also the simplest. Add an `evidence` field to each item: a verbatim quote from the source that supports it. Then check, in deterministic code, that the quote actually appears in the source. Anthropic recommends both halves of this in its guidance on reducing hallucinations: ground answers in direct quotes, and have each claim backed by a supporting quote or retracted.

Asking for the quote helps the model (it has to locate support before committing), but the **verification** is what gives you a guarantee. A model can invent a quote as fluently as it invents a date. Your substring check can't be fooled that way: either the words are in the transcript or they aren't.

Make the check strict and simple. Normalize whitespace on both sides (collapse runs of spaces, tabs, and newlines into one space), because transcripts wrap lines unpredictably. Then require an exact substring match. Resist the urge to use fuzzy matching at first. Every relaxation (ignoring case, punctuation, or small word changes) lets more paraphrases through, and a paraphrase is precisely where meaning drifts. If you later find real quotes failing for trivial reasons, such as curly versus straight apostrophes, add that one normalization and measure the effect.

Know the limit, too. A verified quote proves the words exist. It doesn't prove they support the claim: "Someone should book the venue" is real text, but it isn't a commitment by anyone. That gap needs the semantic checks later in this lesson.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="A verifier that quietly passes everything">
Two bugs make evidence checks useless without failing any test you'd think to write. First, an empty quote: `"" in transcript` is always True in Python, so an item with no evidence passes. Check for empty evidence explicitly. Second, verifying against the wrong text: if you chunk a long transcript, verify against the chunk the model saw (or the whole transcript), never against the model's own summary. Test your verifier with items you know should fail.
</Callout>

## Cross-field rules and abstention

Evidence checks look at one field. Many errors only show up when fields are compared with each other or with facts you hold. Write these as plain functions, run after validation, with one named rule each:

- The owner must be someone in the meeting (or in your team directory). An unknown name is either a hallucination or a person you need to resolve.
- A due date can't be before the meeting date. If the transcript says "Friday," resolve it in code from the meeting date, as the project requires, rather than trusting the model's calendar arithmetic.
- If an item says "done," it shouldn't also have a future due date.
- The owner should be the speaker of the quoted line, or be named in it. This one is a heuristic; use it to flag, not to reject.

Each rule is cheap, deterministic, and testable, and each catches a class of error that no amount of prompting reliably prevents.

The other half is giving the model a legal way to say "I don't know." Anthropic's first recommendation for reducing hallucinations is to explicitly allow uncertainty. In a schema, that means **abstention fields**: make `owner` and `due_date` nullable, and add a short reason when they're null ("no one volunteered", "date discussed but not agreed"). Abstentions are useful data. A sudden jump in null owners tells you the transcripts changed or the prompt broke; a reason string tells a reviewer what to check.
</Listen>

## Confidence the model reports is not confidence you can use

The obvious next idea is to ask the model how sure it is: add a `confidence` field and auto-accept anything above 0.9. The research says to be careful. Xiong and colleagues (2023) benchmarked ways of eliciting confidence from black-box models, including GPT-4, and found that models verbalizing their confidence tend to be **overconfident**, possibly imitating how people talk about certainty. Calibration improved with model scale and with methods that combine several sampled answers, but no method was reliably good, especially on tasks needing specialist knowledge.

There's a nuance worth knowing. Kadavath and colleagues (2022), at Anthropic, showed that large models can be well calibrated when asked in the right format, for example estimating the probability that a proposed answer is true. That's a carefully elicited, measured probability, not a number the model writes into a JSON field mid-extraction.

The practical rule follows from your math background: a confidence score is only meaningful once you've measured its **calibration** on your data. Take a labeled set, bucket items by stated confidence, and compute accuracy per bucket. If the 0.9 bucket is right 70 percent of the time, your threshold is lying to you. Until you've done that, treat the field as a weak signal for sorting a review queue, never as a gate.
</Listen>

## Self-consistency: ask several times, trust agreement

A signal that holds up better than self-reported confidence is **agreement across samples**. Wang and colleagues (2022) introduced self-consistency for reasoning: sample several independent chains of thought instead of one, then take the answer most of them reach. On math word problems it raised accuracy substantially (the paper reports gains as large as 17.9 points on the GSM8K benchmark). The intuition: a correct answer can usually be reached in several ways, while errors scatter.

For extraction, the analog is to run the same extraction several times and compare. An action item that appears in five of five runs, with the same owner, is more trustworthy than one that appears once. Disagreement is the useful part: if three runs say the owner is Sam and two say nobody, that item deserves a person's attention. Anthropic's hallucination guidance suggests the same idea, calling it best-of-N verification: inconsistencies across outputs can indicate hallucination.

Two caveats. Agreement isn't truth: a model can be consistently wrong, especially when the transcript is ambiguous in the same way every time. And it multiplies cost by the number of samples, so use it selectively (for high-stakes fields, or for items that already failed a cheap check). Matching items across runs is also real work: two runs may phrase the same task differently, so match on evidence quotes or on owner plus a normalized task, not on exact task text.
</Listen>

## A second model as checker, and why it is not a referee

Item 2 from the exercise needs judgment: does this quote show this person committing to this task? A natural tool is a second model call that receives the transcript, one item, and a narrow question ("Does the evidence show the named owner committing to the task? Answer yes, no, or unclear, with the reason"). This is the LLM-as-judge pattern. Making the question narrow and binary, about one item at a time, is far more reliable than asking a model to "review the extraction."

Know its limits before you rely on it. Zheng and colleagues (2023) found that strong judge models agreed with human preferences over 80 percent of the time, about as often as humans agree with each other, but also documented position bias, verbosity bias, and self-enhancement bias (favoring answers like their own), plus limited reasoning on hard questions. Self-enhancement is the reason to consider a checker from a **different** model family than the extractor: errors that come from the same training are more likely to be shared. A Claude extractor checked by a GPT or Gemini model (or the reverse) gives you more independent errors, though not independent ones.

Above all, a checker is another model output that needs checking. Hamel Husain's practical guide to LLM judges describes the discipline: label a set of examples yourself, treat those human labels as ground truth, and measure the judge's true positive and true negative rates separately rather than one overall agreement number, because raw agreement misleads when most items are fine. If the checker misses half the bad items, it is a speed bump, not a safety net.
</Listen>

## When to route to a person

Human review is not a failure of automation; it's a component you design. Decide the routing policy in advance and write it down:

- **Always review** items that failed a deterministic check, items where the checker said no or unclear, and items where multiple samples disagreed.
- **Review by stakes.** Some fields cost more when wrong. A due date promised to a client or an owner assigned to a sensitive task may deserve review even when every check passes.
- **Audit what you accept.** Randomly sample a few auto-accepted items each week and have a person check them. That sample is your estimate of the error rate in the part of the pipeline nobody else looks at, and it's how you notice drift when transcripts, prompts, or models change.

Track two numbers over time: the share of items sent to review (your cost) and the error rate in the audited auto-accepted items (your risk). Tightening checks moves cost up and risk down; your job is to pick the trade-off on purpose, with evidence. In the studio project, the optional interactive review step is exactly this queue.

On model selection: all of these checks are model-agnostic, which is what makes them valuable. They let you try a cheaper extractor and measure, rather than guess, what it costs you in review volume and audited errors.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Why sampling-based agreement estimates uncertainty">
If you view each extraction run as a draw from the model's output distribution, agreement across N runs estimates how much probability mass that distribution puts on a given answer. That is a real (if biased) uncertainty measure, because it comes from the model's behavior rather than its self-description. It's the same reason Xiong and colleagues found that consistency among multiple responses helped mitigate overconfidence. Two subtleties matter in practice. First, runs aren't independent of your prompt: an ambiguity in the prompt or transcript shifts every draw the same way, so high agreement can coexist with systematic error. Second, on the current Claude API you can't raise the temperature to get more diverse samples, because sampling parameters are fixed; you still get run-to-run variation (lesson 1), and you can add diversity deliberately by varying the prompt or the order of the transcript chunks.
</GoDeeper>

## The checklist you'll use all studio

For any structured extraction: require evidence and verify it as an exact, whitespace-normalized substring, rejecting empty quotes. Encode cross-field rules as named functions with tests. Give every field the source might not cover a legal null, with a reason. Don't gate on self-reported confidence until you've measured its calibration. Use multi-sample agreement and a narrow second-model check where stakes justify the cost, and measure the checker against your own labels. Route the rest to a person, and audit a sample of what you accept. The lab this week builds the first two layers end to end, and the project asks for all of them.
