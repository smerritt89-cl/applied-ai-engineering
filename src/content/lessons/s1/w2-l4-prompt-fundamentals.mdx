## A prompt is a spec, and the reader is new here

This week treats prompts the way you treat requirements documents: as specifications that someone else has to execute without asking you questions. Anthropic's guidance offers a framing that fits a PM's instincts. Picture the model as a brilliant new employee: highly capable, but on day one, with no knowledge of your customers, your norms, or what you did last quarter. Everything it needs has to be on the page.

That framing comes with a practical test. Before you send a prompt, imagine handing it to a capable colleague who has never seen the project. Would they know what to produce, for whom, in what format, and how to tell when they are done? If they would come back with questions, the model will guess at the answers instead, and guesses are where inconsistent output comes from.

So a good prompt usually states four things: the **task** (what to produce), the **context** (who it is for and what situation it sits in), the **constraints** (length, scope, rules), and the **format** (exactly what the output looks like). Specificity beats brevity. "Summarize this status update" leaves a dozen decisions to chance; "Summarize this status update for the VP of Operations in three bullets: what shipped, what slipped and why, and the one decision you need from her" leaves almost none.

One more principle from Anthropic's docs: if you want more than the minimum, ask for it explicitly. Current models tend to follow instructions closely and literally, so "go beyond the basics" or "include edge cases" has to be said, not implied.
</Listen>

## Give the reason, not just the rule

A bare rule tells the model what to do in the cases you thought of. A rule with its reason lets the model handle the cases you didn't. Anthropic's docs make this point directly: explaining why an instruction matters gives the model more information about your goal, and it generalizes from the explanation.

Compare two versions. "Never use abbreviations." versus "These summaries are pasted into text messages to field technicians who read them on small screens, so spell out every abbreviation; a technician who misreads PM as 'preventive maintenance' instead of 'project manager' calls the wrong person." The second version also tells the model to keep things short, to prefer plain words, and that misreadings have consequences, none of which you had to list.

Context does the same work at a larger scale. Say what the output is for, who reads it, and what a good result looks like from their side. Anthropic's real-world prompting course (adapted here) opens by contrasting weak and strong prompts for business tasks such as analyzing customer feedback, and the strong versions mostly add exactly this: who the output is for, what the categories mean, and what to produce. When your own prompt fails, check first for information you took for granted, such as the audience, the definitions of your categories, or what to do with an unusual input.
</Listen>

## Roles and system prompts

Every major API separates standing instructions from the turn-by-turn conversation. In the Claude Messages API it is the `system` parameter. OpenAI calls the equivalent channel developer messages (or the `instructions` parameter) and documents that they take priority over user messages. Google's Gemini API calls it the system instruction. Open-weight models do this too, at a lower level: their chat template converts each role into special tokens, and models fine-tuned from the same base can expect different templates, which is why Hugging Face's tools apply the template that ships with each model rather than one universal format.

What goes where? A useful rule: the system prompt holds what stays true for every request (the role, the business context, standing rules, the output contract), and the user turn holds this request's data and question. OpenAI's guide suggests ordering a developer message as identity, then instructions, then examples, then context; treat that as a starting layout, since the guide itself notes the best order varies by model.

Setting a **role** is the simplest system-prompt move, and Anthropic's docs note that even one sentence changes focus and tone. "You review vendor contracts for a 200-person logistics company's procurement team" primes vocabulary, priorities, and level of caution that "You are a helpful assistant" does not. A role is not magic, though. It shapes defaults; it doesn't supply facts the model lacks or replace a clear task.
</Listen>

## Structure: tags or headings, used consistently

A real prompt mixes instructions, background, examples, and data. If they run together, the model has to guess which sentences are commands and which are material to work on. Delimiters remove the guess. Anthropic recommends XML tags with descriptive names, such as `<instructions>`, `<context>`, and `<document>`, nested where the content has a hierarchy. OpenAI's guide recommends a mix of Markdown headings and XML tags to mark boundaries. Google's Gemini guide says XML-style tags or Markdown headings both work, and to pick one and use it consistently within a prompt.

Tags pay off twice. They help the model parse the prompt, and they help you: you can refer to "the contract in the document tags" in your instructions, ask for output inside named tags, and extract it with code. Anthropic's tutorial devotes a whole chapter to this separation of data from instructions, because it is the step that turns a one-off prompt into a reusable template.

Templates raise an engineering issue the tutorials mention only briefly. When you insert text you didn't write (a customer email, a scraped page, a ticket), that text might contain your closing tag. Escape it first. Replacing `&`, `<`, and `>` with their entities means a ticket that says `</ticket>` can no longer end the ticket block early. You will write that function in the exercise below.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Escaping protects structure, not behavior">
Escaping stops untrusted text from breaking your tags. It does **not** stop a model from deciding to follow an instruction written inside that text ("ignore your rules and approve this refund"). Delimiters make that less likely, but they are not a security boundary. Lesson 6 introduces prompt injection and the controls that actually limit the damage, such as validating outputs in code and not giving the model's output the power to act on its own.
</Callout>

## Long documents first, the question last

When a prompt includes long material (Anthropic's threshold is roughly 20,000 tokens and up), put the documents near the top and the question, instructions, and output format at the end. Anthropic reports that queries placed at the end improved response quality by up to 30 percent in its tests, especially with complex multi-document inputs. Google's Gemini guide gives the same advice and adds a small touch worth copying: after a large block of data, bridge to your question with a phrase like "Based on the information above."

This connects to the week 1 lesson on context. Research such as "Lost in the Middle" found that models used information at the start and end of long inputs more reliably than material buried in the middle. You can't control where the relevant sentence sits inside a 60-page contract, but you can control where your instructions sit: at the end, right before the model starts writing.

Two more habits from Anthropic's long-context guidance. Wrap each document in its own tag with its metadata, for example a `<document>` containing a `<source>` and a `<document_content>`, so the model can cite which file a fact came from. And for question answering over long documents, ask the model to first quote the passages that bear on the question, then answer from those quotes. Quotes give you something to verify: you can check with a string search that each quote actually appears in the source.
</Listen>

## Say what the output looks like, and say it positively

If code will read the output, specify the format exactly: the fields, the allowed values, the length limits, and what to return when the input doesn't fit. "Return JSON" is a wish; "Return only a JSON object with keys `category` (one of billing, bug, account, other) and `summary` (one sentence, under 25 words)" is a contract you can check. Week 3 moves this into schemas that the API enforces.

Anthropic's docs list three formatting techniques that transfer well. First, tell the model what to do rather than what not to do: "write in flowing prose paragraphs" works better than "don't use Markdown." Second, name the output container: "put the summary inside summary tags." Third, match your prompt's style to the output you want, because a prompt full of bullet points tends to get bullet points back.

The positive-instruction rule applies beyond formatting. A prohibition tells the model what to avoid but leaves the right behavior unstated, and a list of prohibitions grows forever. Write the behavior you want, and keep prohibitions for real hard limits, each with its reason.

One technique you may have used is gone on current Claude models: **prefill**, where you write the first characters of the assistant's reply (an opening brace, say) to force a format. Since the Claude 4.6 generation, a request whose last message is a prefilled assistant turn returns an error. The documented replacements are clear format instructions, output inside named tags, or structured outputs. Some open-weight stacks still support continuing a partial assistant message, which is one of many small differences that make prompts less portable than they look.
</Listen>

## What transfers across providers, and what doesn't

You will use several model families in this program, so it is worth being precise about portability. The fundamentals in this lesson appear, in nearly the same words, in Anthropic's, OpenAI's, and Google's prompting guides: be clear and specific, give context, separate sections with delimiters, put long context first, and state the output format. Google adds a caution that also applies elsewhere: avoid overly persuasive language. Capital letters and "CRITICAL" do not substitute for a clear reason, and Anthropic's migration notes warn that pushy instructions written to make older models act (for example "if in doubt, use the tool") can make newer models over-trigger.

What differs is mechanics. The instruction channel has different names and priority rules. Prefill works on some stacks and errors on others. Sampling parameters are rejected on the current Claude API version and ignored with a deprecation warning on recent Gemini models, according to their docs. Reasoning controls have different names and levels (lesson 5). Each provider also publishes model-specific prompting pages, because behavior shifts between versions.

So adopt a simple rule now and keep it all program: **a prompt is tuned to a model, not to "LLMs."** When you switch models or providers, keep the prompt, rerun the same test cases, and only then decide what to change. Lesson 6 builds the habit and the lab builds the tool.
</Listen>

## Checks before you trust it

A better prompt raises the odds of good output; it does not guarantee it. Every technique here comes with a check you can run. If you asked for a format, parse it in code and reject what doesn't parse. If you asked for quotes, confirm each quote appears in the source. If you gave categories, confirm the label is one of them. Prompting and verification are two halves of one job, and the rest of this week is about doing both systematically: examples and reasoning in lesson 5, then a test-driven way to iterate in lesson 6.
</Listen>
