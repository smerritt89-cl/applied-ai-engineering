## Why not just train everything?

Full fine-tuning updates every weight, and the optimizer has to keep state for every weight. A back-of-envelope count shows the problem. With mixed-precision training and the Adam optimizer, a common layout stores, per parameter, 16-bit weights and 16-bit gradients (2 bytes each), a 32-bit master copy of the weights (4 bytes), and two 32-bit Adam moment estimates (8 bytes): about 16 bytes per parameter before activations. For a 7B model that is roughly 112 GB, which is more than any single consumer GPU. Your setup may differ (8-bit optimizers, no master copy), but the order of magnitude is the point.

The papers you'll read today report the same pressure at scale. Hu and colleagues (2021) cut training memory for GPT-3 175B from 1.2 TB to 350 GB with LoRA. Dettmers and colleagues (2023) report that full 16-bit fine-tuning of a 65B model needs more than 780 GB of GPU memory, and that QLoRA brings it under 48 GB.

There is also a storage and operations problem. A full fine-tune produces a full copy of the model per task. With LoRA, the GPT-3 checkpoint for a task shrank from 350 GB to about 35 MB in the paper's setup, so you keep one base model and many small adapters, and swap them per request or per customer. Lesson 11 shows servers that do exactly that.
</Listen>

## The low-rank update

LoRA freezes each pretrained weight matrix W0, of shape d by k, and learns an additive update constrained to low rank: W0 + delta W, with delta W = B A, where B is d by r, A is r by k, and the rank r is much smaller than d and k. The forward pass for an input x becomes h = W0 x + s B A x, where the scale s is alpha divided by r.

Four details carry most of the practical weight.

**Initialization.** A starts random (Gaussian in the paper; PEFT's default uses a Kaiming-uniform initialization) and B starts at zero, so B A is zero and training begins exactly at the pretrained model.

**Parameter count.** The adapter has r times (d plus k) parameters instead of d times k. For a 4096 by 4096 matrix at r = 8 that is 65,536 instead of about 16.8 million, under 0.4 percent.

**The alpha over r scaling.** The paper sets alpha to the first rank it tries and doesn't tune it, and explains why: with Adam, tuning alpha is roughly the same as tuning the learning rate, and dividing by r reduces how much you must retune when you change r. PEFT implements alpha over r by default and offers `use_rslora`, which divides by the square root of r instead and is described as making higher ranks more effective. Frameworks differ: MLX-LM takes a direct `scale` (default 20.0) rather than alpha. Convert before comparing configurations.

**No inference cost.** After training you can compute W0 + s B A once and store it. The merged layer is an ordinary dense layer, so a merged model is exactly as fast as the base model. Or keep adapters separate and swap them, at a small runtime cost.
</Listen>

## A worked count on a real model

Take Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, the model in this week's lab. Its config lists a hidden size of 896, 24 decoder blocks, 14 query heads and 2 key-value heads with head dimension 64 (896 divided by 14), and an MLP intermediate size of 4,864. The model page reports 494,032,768 parameters.

Grouped-query attention makes the shapes unequal, which is the first thing people get wrong. Per block, as (inputs, outputs):

- q_proj: (896, 896), and o_proj: (896, 896)
- k_proj and v_proj: (896, 128), because 2 key-value heads times 64
- gate_proj and up_proj: (896, 4864), and down_proj: (4864, 896)

At r = 16 on all seven, one block adds 16 times (2 times 1,792 plus 2 times 1,024 plus 3 times 5,760), which is 366,592 parameters. Over 24 blocks: **8,798,208**, about 1.8 percent of the base model. Adapting only q_proj and v_proj at r = 8, the paper's original style, gives 540,672.

Treat this arithmetic as a check you run every time. Before training, predict the count; after building the model, compare with what the library reports (PEFT's `print_trainable_parameters` or `get_nb_trainable_parameters`, or MLX-LM's printout). When the authoring run of this week's lab built the adapter with MLX-LM, it reported 8.798M trainable parameters, matching the hand count. A mismatch means the wrong modules were targeted, which silently changes the experiment.

## Why a low-rank update is enough (when it is)

The motivating evidence came first from Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer, and Gupta (2020), building on a method by Li and colleagues (2018): train a model only inside a random low-dimensional subspace of its parameter space, and find the smallest dimension that reaches 90 percent of full fine-tuning performance. That smallest dimension is the task's **intrinsic dimension**. Their headline result: optimizing only 200 parameters, randomly projected into the full space, let RoBERTa reach 90 percent of full-parameter performance on the MRPC paraphrase task. They also found that pretraining lowers intrinsic dimension and that, after a fixed amount of pretraining, larger models tend to have lower intrinsic dimension.

Hu and colleagues turned that into a hypothesis about the weight update: the change a task needs has low **intrinsic rank**. On GPT-3 they found ranks as small as 1 or 2 sufficed when adapting the query and value projections on their benchmarks. Their analysis section adds a mechanism. The learned update correlates with the pretrained weights more than a random matrix does, but it emphasizes directions the pretrained weights do *not* emphasize, and amplifies them by a large factor (about 21.5 at r = 4 in one layer they studied). In their words, the adaptation amplifies features that were already learned but not emphasized.

Now the counterweight, because the early results were on modest tasks. Biderman and colleagues (2024) compared LoRA and full fine-tuning on code and math, with around 100,000 instruction pairs and with 20 billion tokens of continued pretraining. LoRA substantially underperformed full fine-tuning in their standard low-rank settings; full fine-tuning learned weight changes with rank 10 to 100 times higher than typical LoRA ranks. It also forgot less. Thinking Machines (2025) reached a reconciling picture: LoRA matched full fine-tuning on small-to-medium instruction and reasoning datasets, and fell behind when the dataset exceeded the adapter's capacity.

The working rule: a narrow behavior learned from thousands of examples (a format, a labeling scheme, a style) is well within LoRA's comfort zone. Teaching a model a large new domain from billions of tokens is not, and needs higher ranks or full fine-tuning.
</Listen>

## QLoRA: train adapters on a 4-bit base

LoRA shrinks the optimizer state, but the frozen base weights still sit in GPU memory at 16 bits. QLoRA (Dettmers and colleagues, 2023) stores the frozen base in 4 bits and backpropagates through it into 16-bit LoRA adapters. Three techniques make that work.

**4-bit NormalFloat (NF4).** Pretrained weights are roughly zero-centered and normally distributed. Quantile quantization chooses the 16 levels so that each bin holds the same expected number of values; NF4 computes those levels once from the quantiles of a standard normal, normalized to the range minus 1 to 1. Weights are quantized in blocks of 64, each with its own absolute-max scaling constant, so every block is mapped onto that fixed grid. The authors describe NF4 as information-theoretically optimal for zero-centered normally distributed data, and their experiments found it better than 4-bit float.

**Double quantization.** Those per-block constants cost memory: one 32-bit constant per 64 weights is 0.5 bits per parameter. QLoRA quantizes the constants themselves to 8-bit floats in blocks of 256, cutting the overhead to about 0.127 bits per parameter, a saving of about 0.373 bits, roughly 3 GB on a 65B model.

**Paged optimizers.** Optimizer state is allocated in NVIDIA unified memory, so when a long batch causes a memory spike, pages move to CPU memory instead of crashing the run. The paper notes that paging only occurs on long-sequence mini-batches and that it didn't measure the slowdown systematically.

During each forward and backward pass, a layer's NF4 weights are dequantized to 16-bit (BFloat16 in the paper) for the matrix multiply; gradients are computed only for the adapters. The paper also found that applying LoRA to all linear layers, not just attention, was needed to match 16-bit full fine-tuning in their experiments.
</Listen>

## Where QLoRA runs, and what "QLoRA" means on a Mac

The NF4 recipe in the Hugging Face stack comes from the bitsandbytes library, configured with `BitsAndBytesConfig` (`load_in_4bit`, `bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4"`, `bnb_4bit_use_double_quant`, and `bnb_4bit_compute_dtype`). Transformers' documentation lists bitsandbytes support for NVIDIA CUDA GPUs, Intel GPUs and Gaudi, and CPUs. Apple Silicon is not on that list.

On a Mac, MLX-LM offers the same idea by a different route: if you point `mlx_lm.lora` at a quantized model, it trains LoRA adapters on top of it, and its docs call that QLoRA. MLX's default quantization is affine and group-wise (4 bits, groups of 64, with a scale and bias per group), not NF4. Same pattern, different quantizer, so don't expect identical numbers across the two stacks.

Is QLoRA worth it for the 0.5B model in this week's lab? Not for memory: its bf16 weights are about 1 GB. Use it there to learn the API and to measure the quality difference. It starts to matter at 7B and above, where it decides whether a run fits on the GPU you have.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Comparing or merging adapters across settings that don't match">
Three quiet mismatches cause most LoRA confusion. **Scale conventions:** PEFT's alpha 32 at r 16 is a scale of 2.0; MLX-LM's default `scale` is 20.0 with a default learning rate of 1e-5. Because Adam normalizes gradient magnitudes, scale and learning rate trade off, so compare the product, not either number alone. **Merging into a quantized base:** PEFT's docs warn that merging on a quantized model re-quantizes the base weight, so the merged result isn't exactly the adapter's behavior. Merge into the 16-bit base and re-evaluate. **Target modules:** "all-linear" in PEFT excludes the output layer; MLX-LM adapts all linear layers in the last 16 blocks by default. Count trainable parameters in both before you compare results.
</Callout>

## Target modules and rank: decide with a sweep

Advice on these two choices varies a lot. The original paper adapted attention only, for simplicity. QLoRA found all linear layers necessary to match full fine-tuning. Thinking Machines found MLP layers mattered most and attention-only adapters underperformed even at matched parameter counts. PEFT, if you don't specify targets, picks defaults by architecture, typically query and value. Hugging Face's LLM course suggests ranks from 4 to 32, starting at 4 to 8, with alpha about twice the rank. Maxime Labonne's course lists ranks of 16 to 128 with alpha one to two times the rank. The spread is itself information: the right values depend on the task and the data.

So treat them as experiment parameters. A defensible default for a narrow task is `target_modules="all-linear"`, r = 16, alpha = 32 (scale 2), and the learning rate from lesson 7's guidance. Then run a small sweep: r in 8, 16, and 64, retuning the learning rate if needed (Thinking Machines found the optimum nearly independent of rank under the 1 over r scaling, which makes this cheap). Compare on the frozen validation set with the task metric, plus your general-ability regression set. Pick the smallest configuration within noise of the best, because smaller adapters train faster, forget less, and are cheaper to serve.

Model selection belongs in the same table. If a rank-64 adapter on a 0.5B model still trails a 3B model's rank-16 adapter, the base model was the bottleneck, and no LoRA setting fixes that. Week 4 adds the frontier-model comparison that tells you whether any of it beats a well-prompted API call.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="LoRA as constrained optimization, and the gradient at initialization">
The set of updates LoRA can represent is exactly the matrices of rank at most r, which is a smooth manifold away from lower ranks. If you already knew the full-fine-tuning update delta W, the best rank-r approximation in Frobenius norm would be its truncated SVD (Eckart and Young). LoRA doesn't approximate a known delta W; it optimizes the loss directly over the factorized parameters B and A, a non-convex problem with a symmetry (B A equals B G times G inverse A for any invertible r by r matrix G). Hu and colleagues probed the result empirically: the top singular directions learned at r = 8 and r = 64 overlapped strongly, which is why tiny ranks did well on their tasks. Write g for the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer output and s for alpha over r. Then the gradient with respect to B is s times g times (A x) transposed, and with respect to A is s times B transposed times g times x transposed. At initialization B is zero, so only B moves on the first step; the gradient with respect to B is the full-weight gradient g x transposed projected onto the random row space of A. LoRA's early training is a random-subspace method, which is the bridge back to Aghajanyan's intrinsic dimension.
</GoDeeper>

## Checks and balances for adapter training

The adapter changes a few million numbers you will never read, so verify behavior, not parameters. Predict and confirm the trainable parameter count. Score the base model before training, on the same frozen test set and with the same decoding settings you'll use after. Compare an adapter with its merged form on a handful of inputs: outputs should match to within numerical precision (exactly, in 16-bit; approximately, if you merged into a quantized base). Re-run the evaluation after any change of quantization, because 4-bit storage changes the function slightly. And keep the general-ability regression set from lesson 7 in every comparison, since a sweep that only measures the target task will happily choose the configuration that forgot the most. Finally, keep the comparison honest across stacks and hardware: when a QLoRA run on one machine and a LoRA run on another disagree, check the trainable parameter counts, the scale and learning rate, and the quantizer before concluding anything about the method.
</Listen>
