## The question your benchmark has to answer

By the end of week 3 you have a LoRA adapter that seems to work. Your team is about to ask a concrete question: should the action-item extractor run on this small model, on your own hardware, instead of calling a frontier model's API? The answer depends on four numbers and how sure you are of each: quality, cost per task, latency, and the kinds of mistakes each system makes.

A benchmark that answers this is a controlled experiment, and the controls are where most comparisons go wrong. You need at least three systems. The **frontier model** is the incumbent or the alternative. The **fine-tuned model** is the candidate. The **untuned base model**, served the same way, is the control: it tells you how much of the candidate's quality came from fine-tuning and how much was already in the base model. Without it, a reader can't tell whether your training run did anything.

You built most of the machinery in Studio 3: a versioned test set, code graders, paired statistics, a decision written before you run. This lesson adapts it to the situation fine-tuning creates. The candidate was trained on data you made, so contamination is a live risk. The systems are served differently, so cost and latency need their own accounting. And extraction outputs have structure, so a single pass or fail hides most of what you need to know.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## One frozen test set, one metric, for everyone

Start with the rule that makes every other number meaningful: every system is scored on the **same frozen test set** with the **same scoring code**. Frozen means the cases and gold labels are fixed before any system is run, stored in the repository, and identified by a fingerprint (a hash of ids, inputs, and labels). Every recorded run stores the fingerprint it was scored against, and your scorer refuses to compare runs whose fingerprints differ. That one check catches the most common silent error in comparisons: someone fixes three gold labels between runs, and the "improvement" is the label fix.

The test set must also be one that **no system was tuned on**. For the fine-tuned model that means no test case, and no near-duplicate of one, appears in the training data. Deng and colleagues (2023) document how often public benchmarks leak into training corpora and how much that inflates scores; your own pipeline can leak the same way at small scale. If training and test examples were both sampled from the same week of meetings, a model can score well by recognizing recurring phrases. Split by source (by meeting, by customer, by month) rather than by row, and run a near-duplicate check between the splits before training. For the frontier model, "not tuned on" means you didn't iterate its prompt against the test cases. Tune prompts on a dev split, then run the test split once.

Same inputs does not mean same prompt. The fine-tuned model expects the short format it was trained on; the frontier model does best with instructions and a few examples. Give each system its reasonable best prompt, with a comparable tuning budget, and record each prompt version with its results. What you must hold constant is the input each system sees, the output schema, and the scoring.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Test contamination you created yourself">
The classic version: you generate synthetic training data with a frontier model, seeded from the same transcripts you later label as the test set. The fine-tuned model then scores suspiciously well on exactly those meetings. Prevent it structurally. Freeze and fingerprint the test split first, keep its source documents out of every data-generation step, and run an n-gram or embedding similarity check between training examples and test inputs. If the fine-tuned model beats the frontier model by a surprising margin, check contamination before you celebrate.
</Callout>

## Scoring extraction: per-field accuracy and exact match

An extraction has fields, and fields fail independently. For the action-item task in this studio, each case has a gold record with four fields: whether the snippet contains an action item, the owner, the resolved due date, and the priority. Report **per-field accuracy** (the share of cases where that field matches gold) and **exact match** (the share where every field matches). Exact match is the number that answers "how often is the record fully right," which is usually what a downstream user cares about. Per-field accuracy tells you where the errors are, which is what you need to improve anything.

Three scoring decisions deserve to be written down before you score anything.

**Normalization.** Decide what counts as formatting and what counts as an error. Case and surrounding whitespace in a name are formatting. A date one day off is an error, not a formatting difference. Write these rules as code, apply them to every system, and don't revise them after seeing which system they favor.

**Nulls.** A field the source doesn't support should be null in the gold record. A prediction of null matches a gold null; an invented value against a gold null is wrong. This is how the benchmark rewards abstention, the habit you built in Studio 1.

**Failures stay in the denominator.** An output that doesn't parse, fails schema validation, times out, or is refused is a wrong answer on every field. Dropping failures before computing accuracy rewards the system that fails most often, and small models fail to produce valid structure more often than frontier models unless you constrain their decoding (lesson 11). Report the parse rate as its own column so the reader sees it.

For lists of items (a whole transcript in, many action items out), you also need a matching step between predicted and gold items before field scoring, for example by evidence quote or by owner plus a normalized task. The lab keeps one candidate item per case so the scoring stays simple; the project asks you to handle full lists.

## Intervals: reuse what you built in Studio 3

A fine-tuning project usually ends with a test set of a hundred to a few hundred cases, because every case needs a careful label. At that size, sampling noise is large. With 150 cases and an exact-match rate near 80 percent, a 95 percent interval for one system spans roughly plus or minus 6 to 7 points. Two systems a few points apart are not distinguishable from their separate intervals alone.

The fix is the one from Studio 3, lesson 7: compare systems **paired**, on the same cases. For each case, compute the difference in score between the two systems (1 minus 0, 0 minus 0, and so on), then bootstrap the mean of those differences by resampling cases with replacement. Pairing helps because cases differ in difficulty and both systems tend to find the same cases hard. Miller's paper on error bars for evals (2024), and Anthropic's companion post, recommend exactly this: report paired differences, because the correlation between systems' per-question scores shrinks the variance of the difference.

Then say what the interval supports. If the paired interval for "fine-tuned minus frontier" is plus 1 to plus 12 points, you can say the fine-tuned model is better on this distribution of cases. If it is minus 2 to plus 9, the honest claim is "no detectable loss, and possibly a gain," which may still be enough to switch if the small model is much cheaper. If the lower bound is minus 8, you can't rule out a real loss, and you need more cases or a different decision. Write the margin you will accept before you run, as you did for the CI gate in Studio 3.

Two more habits carry over. If your cases come in clusters (several snippets from the same meeting), resample whole clusters, because cases within a meeting are correlated. And if you run each system more than once, report the spread across runs separately; repeated runs of the same case are not extra cases.
</Listen>

## Cost per 1,000 tasks, including the hardware

Quality differences are often small and uncertain; cost differences are often large and certain. Compute **cost per 1,000 tasks** for each system, because per-request prices are too small to reason about and per-month totals depend on volume.

For an **API model**, cost per task is input tokens times the input price plus output tokens times the output price, using the token counts you recorded during the benchmark, not an estimate. Include everything a task actually consumes: system prompt and few-shot examples (often most of the input), retries after validation failures, and any reasoning tokens the model bills as output. Check the provider's pricing page on the day you write the report. As of this lesson's review date, Anthropic lists Claude Haiku 5.5 at $0.10 and $0.50 per million input and output tokens and Claude Sonnet 5.5 at $2 and $10, with a 50 percent discount for batch processing; those numbers change, which is why your report records the date and the source.

For a **self-hosted model**, the unit is GPU time, and the formula is the GPU's hourly price divided by the tasks it completes per hour, times 1,000. Two inputs are easy to get wrong. **Throughput** must be measured, at the batch size and concurrency you will actually run (lesson 11 explains why throughput depends so much on batching). **Utilization** is the share of paid hours that do useful work. A rented GPU bills whether or not requests arrive; Hugging Face Inference Endpoints, for example, bills per minute while an endpoint is running and lists one NVIDIA L4 on AWS at $0.80 per hour at the time of writing. If your traffic keeps the GPU busy 20 percent of the time, your effective cost per task is five times the busy-GPU figure. Scale-to-zero and batch scheduling change utilization, so say which you assume.

Then name the costs that aren't per task: engineering time to build and maintain serving, monitoring, on-call, and retraining when the task drifts. You don't have to put a dollar figure on them, but a report that compares only GPU-hours to API tokens understates the cost of self-hosting.
</Listen>

## Latency, measured the way users feel it

Report latency as percentiles, at least **p50** (the median) and **p95** (the slowest one in twenty), because averages hide the tail and users notice the tail. For interactive features, measure **time to first token** separately from total time; for extraction, where your code needs the whole JSON object, total time is what matters.

Measure under realistic conditions, or the numbers mean little. Call the API from the region your application runs in. Load the self-hosted server with the concurrency you expect in production, because a server that answers one request in 300 milliseconds may take several seconds per request when forty arrive at once. Warm up first, so model loading and cache misses don't land in your sample. Record the hardware, the serving engine and its version, quantization, and batch settings alongside the numbers. Independent benchmarkers such as Artificial Analysis publish their latency definitions for exactly this reason: two "latency" numbers measured differently can't be compared.

Latency is also where the systems often differ most. A small model on a dedicated GPU, close to your application, can answer in well under a second; a frontier API call commonly takes longer, and its tail depends on load you don't control. Whether that matters depends on the product: a batch job that processes yesterday's meetings overnight doesn't care, and an inline suggestion while someone types does.
</Listen>

## Failure analysis: read the disagreements

The numbers say how often each system is wrong. Only reading the errors says why, and the why decides what you do next. Pull every case where the systems disagree, plus every case where both are wrong, and label each error with a short category: missed item, invented item, wrong owner, date arithmetic, priority convention, schema failure, and so on. This is the error analysis loop from Studio 3, lesson 2, applied to a comparison.

Patterns you are likely to see in a fine-tuning comparison:

- **Convention errors favor the fine-tuned model.** If priority follows a house rule ("anything a client is waiting on is high"), a model trained on hundreds of labeled examples learns the rule; a frontier model with a few-shot prompt has to infer it. This is the narrow, learnable kind of task where fine-tuning tends to win.
- **Judgment and world-knowledge errors favor the frontier model.** Ambiguous ownership, unusual phrasing, or a snippet unlike anything in training is where a larger, more general model holds up better.
- **Schema failures cluster in small models** unless decoding is constrained. If most of the fine-tuned model's errors are parse failures, the fix is in serving (lesson 11), not training.
- **Shared errors point at the labels or the task.** If every system gets a case wrong in the same way, read the gold label again. Sometimes the label is wrong, and sometimes the snippet is genuinely ambiguous and should be marked as such in the next version of the test set (never silently edited in this one).

Report a small table of error categories by system. It is the most persuasive part of the write-up for a reader who doesn't trust aggregate scores, and it tells you whether a second round of training data would help.
</Listen>

<Callout type="claim" title="Claim: small fine-tuned models match or beat frontier models on narrow tasks">
**Who and when:** the Predibase team (Zhao and colleagues) in the technical report [LoRA Land](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00732), April 2024, whose title says 310 fine-tuned models "rival GPT-4." The same claim circulates widely among open-model practitioners.

**Evidence for.** LoRA Land fine-tuned 10 base models of 2 to 7 billion parameters on 31 tasks with 4-bit LoRA. The fine-tuned models beat their base models by 34 points and GPT-4 by 10 points on average, and the best fine-tuned model beat GPT-4 on 24 of the 31 tasks. Bucher and Martini (2024) found fine-tuned BERT-style classifiers beat zero-shot GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Claude Opus on every text-classification task they tested. Hsieh and colleagues (2023) trained a 770-million-parameter model, using rationales, that outperformed few-shot PaLM 540B on one benchmark with 80 percent of the training data.

**Evidence against, or limits.** In LoRA Land, GPT-4 was prompted simply (zero or one example, no chain of thought), and it still won on the broader tasks, including Python coding and MMLU. The comparison point was a 2023 model, and frontier models have improved since. Gudibande and colleagues (2023) showed the failure mode on the other side: models fine-tuned to imitate ChatGPT fooled crowd raters on style but closed little of the capability gap on targeted evaluations, especially on tasks not covered by the imitation data. And averages over many tasks say nothing about yours.

**How to read it.** The evidence supports a narrower claim: on a well-defined task with good labeled data and a fixed output format, a fine-tuned small model often matches a prompted frontier model, at lower cost per task. Whether that holds for your task is what your benchmark is for.
</Callout>

<GoDeeper title="Why the paired interval is narrower, in one line of algebra">
For per-case scores A and B, the variance of the mean difference is (Var A + Var B minus 2 Cov(A, B)) divided by n. An unpaired comparison effectively assumes the covariance is zero. In practice it is strongly positive, because hard cases are hard for every system, so the paired variance is smaller, sometimes by a lot. The bootstrap estimates this without the formula: resampling the same case indices for both systems preserves the covariance automatically. Clustered data adds a design effect on top, which is why Miller recommends clustered standard errors when questions share a source document. If you want the original argument for the bootstrap itself, Efron's 1979 paper is short and readable for a mathematician.
</GoDeeper>

## The report, and the decision it supports

Put the results in one table: for each system, the test-set size, parse rate, exact match with its interval, per-field accuracy, cost per 1,000 tasks with its assumptions, and p50 and p95 latency. Below it, the paired differences against the frontier model, the error-category table, and two sentences on what the benchmark doesn't cover (traffic unlike the test set, drift over time, adversarial inputs).

Then write the decision the way a reviewer can check it: "On 150 frozen cases, the fine-tuned model's exact match is within 2 points below to 12 points above the frontier model's. At our expected volume and 20 percent GPU utilization it costs about 30 percent less per 1,000 tasks, and its p95 latency is under a second against over two seconds. We recommend switching the batch pipeline, keeping the frontier model as a fallback for snippets the small model fails to parse, and re-running this benchmark monthly on a fresh sample." That paragraph is the deliverable. The rest of the report is the evidence for it.
</Listen>
