## What you actually get when a model is "open"

When a lab releases an open model, you typically download three things: a set of **weights** (the learned parameters, often tens or hundreds of gigabytes), a **config** describing the architecture, and a **tokenizer** with its chat template. With an inference library you can run it on your own hardware, inspect it, quantize it, and fine-tune it. That is a lot of freedom compared with an API, where you only see text in and text out.

What you usually don't get is how the model was made: the training data, the data filtering code, and the training code. That gap is why careful people say **open-weight** rather than open source. Think of it like receiving a compiled binary under a generous license. You can run it, patch it, and redistribute it, but you can't rebuild it from source, and you can't audit what went into it.

The Open Source Initiative, the body that maintains the Open Source Definition for software, published the **Open Source AI Definition 1.0** in October 2024. It carries the four software freedoms over to AI systems: freedom to **use** the system for any purpose without asking permission, to **study** how it works, to **modify** it for any purpose, and to **share** it, modified or not. To make those freedoms real, it asks for the "preferred form for making modifications," which has three parts: **data information** detailed enough that a skilled person could build a substantially equivalent system (provenance, scope, collection, filtering, and a list of sources), the complete **code** for training and inference, and the **parameters**.

By that standard, most popular open models are open-weight only. A few publishers go further. Ai2 releases its Olmo models under Apache 2.0 along with the Dolma pretraining data and its training code, which is why researchers use Olmo when they need to study how training choices affect behavior.
</Listen>

<Callout type="claim" title="OSI: Llama's license is not open source (February 2025)">
**Who and when:** Jordan Maris, writing on the Open Source Initiative blog on 18 February 2025, argued that Meta's Llama license "is still not Open Source," and called the marketing open washing. [Read the post](https://opensource.org/blog/metas-llama-license-is-still-not-open-source).

**Evidence for:** the license restricts who may use the model (a separate license is needed above 700 million monthly active users), incorporates an acceptable use policy that limits fields of use, and the Llama 4 use policy withholds rights to the multimodal models from individuals and companies based in the EU. Restricting persons and fields of use conflicts with the OSI's software definition.

**Evidence against, or nuance:** for most companies the weights are downloadable and commercially usable at no cost, which is far more open than an API. Whether you call that "open" is a labeling argument. The practical point for you: the license text, not the label on the model page, decides what you may build.
</Callout>

## Permissive licenses: Apache 2.0 and MIT

Many open models now ship under the same licenses as ordinary software, and those are the easiest to work with.

**Apache 2.0** grants a perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free right to use, copy, modify, sublicense, and distribute the work (section 2). It also grants an explicit **patent license** from each contributor, which ends for anyone who sues claiming the work infringes a patent (section 3). Its conditions are about notices: when you redistribute, include the license, mark files you changed, keep the copyright and attribution notices, and carry forward the contents of any NOTICE file (section 4). It grants no trademark rights (section 6), so you can ship a fine-tune but not call it by the publisher's brand. At the time of writing, OpenAI's gpt-oss models, Google's Gemma 4, Ai2's Olmo models, Qwen3.8-27B, and several Mistral models (including Mistral Small 4 and Mistral Large 3) use Apache 2.0.

**MIT** is shorter. It permits essentially anything, including selling, on the single condition that you keep the copyright and permission notice, and it disclaims all warranties. Unlike Apache 2.0, its text contains no explicit patent grant. DeepSeek's recent model cards state that both the repository and the weights are MIT licensed, and Microsoft's Phi models use MIT.

One caution that applies to both: the license on the weights says nothing about the **outputs** or the **training data**. A permissively licensed model can still produce text that infringes someone's rights, and it can be trained on data with its own terms. License review covers what you may do with the artifact; your product still needs its own checks on what the model says.
</Listen>

## Custom licenses: read for the conditions

The other large group is custom licenses written by the publisher. They usually allow commercial use, and the risk is in the conditions. Five kinds of clause come up again and again, and they are what you scan for.

**User or revenue thresholds.** The Llama 4 Community License requires a separate license from Meta if your products had more than 700 million monthly active users on the release date. Newer custom licenses use revenue: the Qwen3.8-Max License (for Qwen's largest Qwen3.8 model) requires products above 100 million monthly users or 20 million dollars in monthly revenue to display the model's name, and requires a separate license for model-as-a-service businesses above 50 million dollars of revenue over 12 months.

**Model-as-a-service restrictions.** Several licenses treat hosting the model for others differently from building products with it. Mistral Large 4's Ecosystem Open-Weight License is a modified MIT license under which model-as-a-service businesses need a commercial license. Moonshot's Kimi K3.1-Pro license and MiniMax's Community License have comparable clauses, and MiniMax's base grant covers non-commercial use only, with conditions for commercial use.

**Naming and attribution.** Llama 4 requires you to display "Built with Llama," and a derivative model you distribute must have a name that begins with "Llama."

**Use policies.** Llama 4 incorporates an acceptable use policy; the Gemma Terms of Use (which cover Gemma releases before Gemma 4) incorporate a Prohibited Use Policy, require you to pass the restrictions on to anyone you distribute to, and let Google restrict use remotely or otherwise. Use policies can change after you adopt the model, so note the version you agreed to.

**Regional limits.** The Llama 4 acceptable use policy withholds rights to the multimodal Llama 4 models from individuals and companies whose principal place of business is in the EU (end users of products built on them are exempt). Hugging Face even has a gating setting for this.

Licenses also change between generations. Gemma moved from the Gemma Terms of Use to Apache 2.0 with Gemma 4, and NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 models use OpenMDW 1.1, a permissive license written for models and data. Always read the license for the exact model and version you ship. On the Hugging Face Hub, a repo whose card says `license: other` keeps the actual terms in a LICENSE file and names the license in `license_name`; that file is the one to read.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Treating the Hub's license tag as legal review">
The license badge on a model page comes from the card's metadata, which the uploader writes. Quantized copies and fine-tunes uploaded by third parties sometimes carry the wrong tag, or drop the base model's conditions entirely. Trace every model back to its `base_model` and read the publisher's license, record the exact repo revision you used, and have counsel sign off on custom licenses. The tag is a search filter, not a contract.
</Callout>

## Open model or frontier API?

Teams choose open models for four structural reasons. **Privacy and data control:** prompts and outputs never leave infrastructure you control, which matters for regulated data, contracts that forbid third-party processing, and anything you would not paste into someone else's service. **Offline and edge use:** a model on the device works without a network, on a plane, in a clinic, or in a factory. **Customization:** with the weights you can fine-tune on your data (weeks 2 and 3 of this studio), quantize for your hardware, or constrain decoding however you like. **Version control:** the weights you tested are the weights that run, for as long as you want, with no deprecation schedule.

**Cost at scale** is the fifth reason, and the one most often misjudged. An API charges per token. Self-hosting charges for hardware and operations whether you use it or not. Self-hosting only wins when volume is high and steady enough to keep the hardware busy, and when the API you are comparing against is expensive. Chip Huyen's *AI Engineering* frames this as a build-versus-buy decision that also counts engineering time, monitoring, and the safety work an API provider would otherwise do.

The case for frontier APIs is equally concrete. The strongest frontier models still lead on the hardest reasoning, coding, and agentic tasks; someone else runs, scales, and secures the serving stack; and you get new capabilities without migrating. For most teams the answer is a mix: a frontier API where quality decides, and a small open model where privacy, offline use, latency, or volume decide. The way to choose is the one this program keeps returning to: build a small eval from real examples and measure quality, cost per task, and latency for each candidate.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Why the definition asks for data information, not the data itself">
The OSAID deliberately stops short of requiring the training data to be released. Training sets often contain material that can't legally be redistributed (copyrighted text, personal data), so the definition asks for enough information about the data to build a substantially equivalent system, plus a list of the public and third-party sources. Critics argue that without the data you can't truly study or reproduce a model; supporters argue that a stricter rule would leave almost no qualifying models. For engineering purposes the distinction shows up in two places. Without data information you can't check whether a benchmark leaked into training (Studio 1 covered contamination), and you can't reason about what the model has never seen. When provenance matters, for example in regulated settings, models with released data such as Olmo let you answer questions that an open-weight release can't.
</GoDeeper>

## What a working engineer does with this

On a real team, picking an open model is a small procurement process. Write down the constraints first: where data may go, whether you need offline use, expected volume, and whether you plan to fine-tune or host the model for others. Shortlist two or three models whose licenses fit, using the publisher's license text, not the tag. Record the exact repo and revision, so the model you evaluated is the one you deploy.
