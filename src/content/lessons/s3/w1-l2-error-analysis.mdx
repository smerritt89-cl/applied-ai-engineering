## The highest-value hour in the project

Most teams, asked to "add evals," start by choosing metrics or a tool. Husain and Shankar's evals FAQ argues for the opposite order, calling error analysis "the most important activity in evals": before you decide what to measure, read what your system actually does and find out how it fails.

The reason is that you can't measure what you haven't named. A support bot's real problems are rarely "hallucination" in the abstract. They are things like "tells customers about integrations we don't have" or "tries to fix account lockouts instead of handing off." Those are product-specific, and you only find them by reading. Husain's field guide gives a striking example: in one client's assistant, error analysis showed that three issues accounted for over 60 percent of all problems, which told the team where to spend its time.

The unit you read is a **trace**: the complete record of one interaction. For a chat assistant that is the user message, what was retrieved, any tool calls and their results, and the reply. For an agent it is every step. Anthropic's evals post makes the same point from the other direction: read many transcripts, because a score alone can't tell you whether a failure is the system's fault or the grader's.

This lesson walks through the method: sample traces, write open notes, group them into a taxonomy, count, and decide. Then the lab has you do it on 36 traces from a fictional support bot.
</Listen>

## Step 1: get traces in front of a person

Start by sampling. The FAQ suggests beginning with roughly 100 diverse traces and annotating at least the first 30 yourself. "Diverse" matters more than random: make sure the sample covers your main features, user types, and channels, and include traces users complained about. If you only sample the happy path, you'll learn about the happy path.

Next, remove friction. Husain's field guide calls a simple custom data viewer one of the most valuable investments a team can make: everything about one trace on one screen, a quick pass or fail, a free-text note, and hotkeys to move on. It also says a spreadsheet beats nothing, and for a first pass of a hundred traces a spreadsheet is fine. The lab gives you a CSV template for exactly this.

Then judge each trace with a **binary** label, pass or fail. The FAQ recommends binary judgments over 1 to 5 scales because they force a decision, make labels more consistent between sessions and people, and avoid the drift toward the middle that rating scales invite. If you want to track gradual progress, use several specific binary checks rather than one scale.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## Step 2: open coding

For every failing trace, write a short note about what went wrong. This is **open coding**, a term the FAQ borrows from qualitative research, where it compares the activity to journaling. The rules are few and they matter:

- **Don't start with categories.** Write what you see in plain words: "Says QuickBooks Desktop sync exists; it doesn't." Husain's field guide contrasts this bottom-up approach with top-down lists like "hallucination, toxicity, verbosity," which tend to miss the failures specific to your product.
- **Note the first thing that went wrong.** In a multi-step trace, one early mistake (bad retrieval, a misread question) causes the visible ones downstream. Fixing the first error often fixes the rest.
- **Be specific enough to act on.** "Bad answer" is useless. "Answered about multi-currency when asked about per-line tax" points at a fix.
- **Note good behavior that surprised you.** A bot that correctly says "I can't find that, let me connect you with the team" is worth protecting with a regression test.

This is slow at first and gets fast. It is also where a domain expert earns their keep, because "is this answer correct?" often needs product knowledge an engineer doesn't have.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## Step 3: axial coding into a failure taxonomy

When you have notes on enough failures, read them all and group similar ones. This is **axial coding**: finding the categories that connect your open codes. The output is a **failure taxonomy**, a short list of failure modes, each with a one-line definition precise enough that two people would usually agree on whether a trace shows it. The FAQ calls this grouping step the most important part, and the next step is to count failures in each category.

A good taxonomy for one product usually has five to ten modes. Too few, and a category like "wrong answer" hides two different problems with two different fixes. Too many, and you're back to individual notes. Write the definitions down, because they become the spec for the graders you'll build next week.

An LLM can help here, after you've done the first pass yourself. The FAQ suggests annotating at least 30 traces by hand before letting a model cluster notes or suggest matches, and then accepting or rejecting its suggestions. In the video they explain why the first pass should be a person: the model doesn't know your product's rules, and the act of reading is how *you* learn what failure means. Model choice matters little for this clustering step; any strong general model with enough context to hold your notes will do, and you are the check on its groupings.

Keep going until new traces stop producing new codes or changing your definitions. The FAQ calls this **theoretical saturation**, and treats it as the signal to stop the discovery phase for this cycle (it suggests reviewing at least 100 traces and continuing past that while you're still learning).
</Listen>

## Your criteria will move, and that's expected

Shreya Shankar and colleagues studied people building LLM evaluators in "Who Validates the Validators?" (2024). Their tool, EvalGen, proposes candidate graders (code or LLM prompts) and asks the user to grade a sample of outputs, then picks the graders that best agree with the user's grades. The striking finding was **criteria drift**: people need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs changes their criteria. Some criteria only appeared once participants saw particular outputs.

Two practical consequences follow. First, a rubric written entirely before reading outputs will be incomplete, which is one more argument for doing error analysis first. Second, your taxonomy is a living document. Version it, note when a definition changes, and expect to revise it when the product, the users, or the model changes. Re-labeling a few old traces after a definition change keeps your counts comparable.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## Step 4: count, then decide what to do

With a taxonomy, counting is mechanical: for each failure mode, how many failing traces show it. Count traces, not labels, so a note that mentions the same mode twice doesn't double-count. Divide by the number of traces reviewed to get a rate, and keep the denominator visible; "9 of 36" is more honest than "25 percent" on a sample this small.

Frequency alone isn't priority. An invented refund policy hurts more than an over-polite reply, even if the polite reply is more common. A simple, transparent way to rank is to give each mode a severity (say 1 for cosmetic, 3 for harmful or costly) and sort by rate times severity. The weights are a judgment, so write down who set them and why. The FAQ suggests something similar when combining checks: report release-blocking failures separately from tolerable ones, rather than averaging everything together.

Then decide, mode by mode. Some failures have an obvious, cheap fix (a missing rule in the prompt, a retrieval filter) that you can make and verify on the same traces. Others recur after fixes or need judgment to detect; those deserve an automated eval so you can track them over time. Next week you'll build graders for exactly those modes, and Husain's guide to LLM judges explains how to check a judge against the expert's labels.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Letting the model do the reading for you">
The tempting shortcut is to paste 500 traces into a long-context model and ask for "the main failure modes." You'll get a tidy, plausible list, and it will lean toward generic categories, because the model doesn't know your refund policy, your escalation list, or what your users actually needed. Worse, you'll skip the step where you learn those things. Do the first 30 to 50 traces yourself. Then use a model to speed up clustering and matching, and check its suggestions against your notes. Its output is one more thing to verify.
</Callout>

## Who judges: the benevolent dictator

Error analysis needs a decision-maker for "is this a failure?" The FAQ's recommendation for most small and medium teams is a single domain expert who acts as the **benevolent dictator** of quality: they set the standard, and their labels are the ground truth. The episode covers this at about 25:16. The FAQ's reasoning: one expert avoids endless annotation conflicts, and if you feel you need five experts to judge one interaction, your product's scope may be too broad.

That expert should be whoever best knows what a correct answer looks like for users: often a product manager, a support lead, or a subject-matter specialist, not necessarily an engineer. The FAQ describes engineers and PMs catching different problems (technical versus product failures) and favors a domain expert or PM leading over time. It also calls outsourcing core error analysis to a labeling vendor usually a big mistake, with exceptions for mechanical labels that don't need product context.

When you do have several annotators, measure their agreement before trusting combined labels (the FAQ mentions Cohen's kappa, a chance-corrected agreement statistic), hold alignment sessions on disagreements, and let the expert make the final call. Disagreements are useful: they usually point at a fuzzy definition in your taxonomy.

For model selection, error analysis is also your best comparison tool. When you trial a second model or provider, run the same traces through it and code its failures with the same taxonomy. Two models with the same pass rate can fail in completely different ways, and the ways matter.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Error analysis before LLMs">
Error analysis is older than LLM evals. In classic supervised learning, Andrew Ng taught the same habit: take around a hundred examples the model got wrong, tag each with the reasons, and count, so you know how much fixing each category could improve the system at most before you spend weeks on it. The vocabulary differs (dev set, ceiling on performance) but the logic is identical to this lesson's: count the failures you actually have before choosing what to build.
