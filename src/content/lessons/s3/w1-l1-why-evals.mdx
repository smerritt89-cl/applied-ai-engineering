## When vibes stop working

Every team starts the same way. You write a prompt, try it on a few inputs, read the answers, and adjust. This works well at first, because early problems are obvious and one look tells you whether a change helped. Studio 1 had you do exactly this, plus a little more: the prompt test harness in week 2 was a small eval.

Then it stops working. Hamel Husain describes this arc in his essay "Your AI Product Needs Evals," using Rechat's real estate assistant, Lucy, as the case study. Prompt engineering brought quick gains, then progress stalled. Fixing one failure exposed or caused others. Nobody could see how the system behaved across the full range of requests, and the prompt grew long and fragile. The team's way out was to build evaluation into how they worked.

The mechanism is simple. A product with a dozen behaviors to protect has too many interactions to hold in your head. Each change helps some inputs and hurts others, and spot-checking five examples samples almost none of the space. Without measurement you can't tell progress from churn, and you tend to keep whichever version you looked at last.

An **eval** replaces the spot check with a repeatable measurement: a fixed set of inputs, a way to grade each output, and a number you can compare across versions. This studio teaches you to build them, starting this week with the step most teams skip: looking at your data.
</Listen>

## What an eval is, precisely

Anthropic's engineering post "Demystifying evals for AI agents" gives a vocabulary worth adopting. A **task** is one test case: defined inputs plus success criteria. A **trial** is one attempt at a task; you often run several, because outputs vary. A **grader** is the logic that scores some aspect of an attempt, and one task can have several. The **transcript** is the full record of a trial (outputs, tool calls, reasoning), and the **outcome** is the end state you actually care about, such as whether a refund was really issued.

Before you can grade, you need criteria. Anthropic's eval docs say good success criteria are specific, measurable, achievable, and relevant, and that most real applications need several at once. "The bot should be helpful" fails every one of those tests. "On our held-out set, the bot hands off every request on the escalation list, and invents no product features" passes them. The docs also give three design principles you'll see throughout this studio: make evals task-specific (mirror your real distribution, including edge cases), automate grading where you can, and prefer more cases with a decent automated grader over a few hand-graded ones.

Graders come in three kinds, which week 2 covers in depth. **Code-based** graders (exact match, a regex, a JSON check, "is this quote in the source") are fast and reliable but can't judge nuance. **Model-based** graders (an LLM judge) handle nuance but must themselves be checked against people. **Human** graders are the most flexible and the slowest. A good eval uses the cheapest grader that is trustworthy for each criterion.

## The eval-driven development loop

The working loop has five steps, and it's worth memorizing because every lesson in this studio hangs off it.

1. **Look at data.** Read real outputs (traces) before deciding what to measure. Lesson 2 is entirely about this.
2. **Define failure modes.** Name the ways the system fails, specifically enough that two people would agree on whether a trace shows one.
3. **Measure.** Build a test set and graders for the failure modes that matter most, and record a baseline.
4. **Change one thing.** A prompt edit, a retrieval setting, a model swap. One.
5. **Re-measure** on the same set, compare with the baseline, keep or revert, and go back to step 1 with fresh traces.

Husain's essay frames this as a virtuous cycle of evaluating quality, debugging, and changing the system, and argues that doing all three well is what separates products that keep improving from products that plateau.

"Change one thing" deserves a moment, because it is just experimental design. If you change the prompt and the model together and the score rises, you have one observation and two candidate causes. The eval can tell you the bundle helped; it can't tell you why. On small test sets it may not even tell you that much, because a few cases flipping can be noise. Week 3 makes this quantitative with confidence intervals, following the approach in Evan Miller's "Adding Error Bars to Evals."
</Listen>

## Two shapes of eval

Evals come in two broad shapes, and you need both.

**Unit-test-style evals** are assertions: specific, fast, cheap checks on specific inputs. "The output parses." "Every evidence quote appears in the transcript." "When the user asks to delete their data, the reply offers a handoff." Husain calls these level 1 in his essay and recommends running them on every code change, organized by feature and scenario. One difference from software unit tests: the target pass rate is a product decision, not automatically 100 percent, because some checks cover behavior the model gets right most but not all of the time.

**Benchmark-style evals** are scored sets: a few hundred representative cases, a grader, and an aggregate number (accuracy, pass rate, a failure-mode rate) you compare across versions and models. They answer "is version B better than version A overall?" and "which model should we use?"

Anthropic's post adds a second axis. **Capability evals** target things the system can't yet do well, so they start with low pass rates and give you a hill to climb. **Regression evals** protect things that already work and should stay near 100 percent. When a capability eval's pass rate gets high, its cases "graduate" into the regression suite. An eval that every version passes (a saturated eval) still guards against regressions but no longer tells you how to improve, so you add harder cases.

In practice you start small. The same post suggests 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures as a good beginning, and warns that waiting for a perfect set of hundreds only makes evals harder to start.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Grabbing a generic metric off the shelf">
It is tempting to start with a framework's built-in "helpfulness," "coherence," or similarity score, because it produces a number on day one. Husain and Shankar's evals FAQ argues that such generic metrics usually don't track the failures your users hit and can create false confidence; they suggest using them at most to find interesting traces to read. A score that rises while your support bot keeps inventing features is worse than no score, because it tells everyone things are fine. Derive what you measure from your own failure modes (lesson 2).
</Callout>

## Why public benchmarks don't tell you how a model does on your task

Leaderboards are where most people first meet "evaluation," and Studio 1 already showed you their caveats. Here is the summary in eval terms.

A public benchmark is a fixed set of tasks with a fixed grader. Your task has different inputs, different rules (your refund policy, your escalation list), and different failure modes. Chip Huyen's *AI Engineering* treats public benchmarks as one input to model selection (chapter 4 has a section on navigating them), alongside evaluating candidates on your own application. The reason is distribution: a benchmark tells you how a model does on the benchmark's inputs, and your inputs are not those.

Three specific problems make benchmark numbers weaker evidence than they look. **Contamination:** Deng and colleagues (2023) found signs that benchmark content had leaked into training data for some models, which inflates scores without reflecting real ability. **Leaderboard practices:** Singh and colleagues (2025) documented ways the Chatbot Arena leaderboard's rules let some providers test many private variants and report the best, which distorts rankings. **Grading bugs:** Anthropic's evals post reports that one model's score on the CORE-Bench benchmark rose from 42 to 95 percent after the team fixed grading bugs and loosened a constrained scaffold, so the original number measured the harness as much as the model.

None of this makes benchmarks useless. The evals FAQ says they can help you choose a promising model to start with. That is the right role: shortlist two or three models (say, a Claude, a GPT, and an open-weight model you could self-host) using benchmarks, price, and latency, then let an eval built from your traffic pick the winner. The same eval then tells you, months later, whether a new model release is worth switching to.
</Listen>

<Callout type="claim" title='"Evals are the new PRDs"'>
**Claim.** In the September 2025 Lenny's Podcast episode above (chapter "Why evals are the new PRDs for AI products," about 1:00:51), Husain and Shankar argue that eval criteria, including the prompts for LLM judges, act as a living requirements document for an AI product: they say precisely what the system must and must not do, and they are checked continuously.

**For.** A product requirements document is only as good as its testability, and eval criteria are testable by construction. Anthropic's eval docs push in the same direction: specific, measurable success criteria come before building anything.

**Against.** Evals capture what you thought to test. Shankar and colleagues' own research (lesson 2) finds that people's criteria shift as they read outputs, so an eval suite is a snapshot of current understanding, not a complete spec. Strategy, user research, and scope decisions still need a document.

**Take.** Treat your eval suite as the executable part of the requirements, owned and reviewed like the rest of them.
</Callout>

## What this looks like on a real team

A few working habits make the loop survive contact with a busy team.

**Keep the eval in the repo**, next to the prompt and code it tests, so a change and its eval result are reviewed together. **Give it an owner.** Anthropic's post recommends clear ownership of eval suites and letting domain experts contribute tasks, because they know what "correct" means better than the engineers do. **Start from real failures.** Bug reports, support tickets, and things a reviewer flagged are the best first test cases, because they are known to happen. **Write unambiguous tasks.** Anthropic's test for a good task is that two domain experts would independently reach the same pass or fail verdict; if they wouldn't, fix the task before trusting its score. **Read transcripts, not just scores.** A score drop can be a real regression or a broken grader, and only reading tells you which.

For a PM, this is familiar territory: it is acceptance criteria and a release checklist, made executable. Your leverage is in steps 1 and 2 of the loop, deciding what failure means for users. The rest of this week does exactly that: lesson 2 turns raw traces into a ranked list of failure modes, and lesson 3 turns those into test sets.
</Listen>
