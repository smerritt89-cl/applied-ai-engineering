## Why an engineer needs this lesson

Regulation is usually someone else's job until a launch is blocked or a customer's procurement questionnaire asks what you have done about the EU AI Act. This lesson gives you enough to have that conversation well: the dates, who is responsible for what, and which artifacts an engineering team should produce so that legal and compliance colleagues have something to work with.

Two cautions. First, this is not legal advice, and the details move: the EU amended its law in 2026 and US policy shifted twice in two years. Every date below was checked on 11 November 2026 against the cited text; check again before relying on it. Second, the frontier-model rules that get headlines mostly bind the handful of companies that train the largest models. What binds most teams is less dramatic: transparency duties, risk classification of specific use cases, data protection, and sector law. Knowing which is which saves a lot of anxiety.
</Listen>

## The EU AI Act: structure and the general-purpose AI chapter

The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024. It sorts AI by risk: a short list of **prohibited practices** (Article 5); **high-risk systems** in listed areas such as employment, education, credit, essential services, and law enforcement, which carry the heaviest duties; **transparency duties** for systems that interact with people or generate content (Article 50); and a separate chapter for **general-purpose AI models**.

The general-purpose AI (GPAI) chapter applies to providers of the models themselves. Under Article 53, every GPAI provider must keep technical documentation, give downstream providers enough information to understand the model's capabilities and limits, have a policy to comply with EU copyright law, and publish a summary of the content used for training. Models are presumed to have **systemic risk** when their cumulative training compute exceeds 10 to the 25 floating-point operations (Article 51), and their providers must additionally evaluate and adversarially test them, assess and mitigate systemic risks, report serious incidents to the AI Office, and ensure cybersecurity (Article 55). Open-source models released under qualifying licenses are exempt from some of the documentation duties, but not from the systemic-risk duties.

The European Commission's guidance gives the timeline. GPAI obligations applied from **2 August 2025**. The Commission's enforcement powers, including fines, apply from **2 August 2026**. Providers of models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 have until **2 August 2027**. Alongside the law, the Commission published a voluntary **General-Purpose AI Code of Practice** (July 2025) with chapters on transparency, copyright, and safety and security; providers that sign it can use adherence to demonstrate compliance, and the Commission says it will focus enforcement on monitoring that adherence.

## The 2026 Digital Omnibus: what moved and what did not

In 2026 the EU amended the Act through the **Digital Omnibus on AI**, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published in the Official Journal in July and in force from **27 July 2026**. The changes that matter for planning:

- **High-risk obligations were deferred**: to **2 December 2027** for standalone systems in the Annex III areas (employment, credit, and so on) and to **2 August 2028** for AI embedded in products covered by existing EU product law (Annex I).
- **Generative-content marking got a transition**: generative AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have until **2 December 2026** to meet the machine-readable marking duty in Article 50(2).
- **AI literacy was reworded**: Article 4 now requires providers and deployers to take measures to support AI literacy among their staff, without prescribing a level for any individual.
- **The AI Office's role grew**: it gained exclusive competence over AI systems built on a GPAI model by the same provider, with new supervisory and fining powers.
- **The GPAI chapter's obligations did not move.**

Commentary on the Omnibus disagrees on some details, especially exactly how far transparency duties shifted. When a date matters for your launch, read the consolidated text on EUR-Lex rather than a summary, including this one.
</Listen>

## Model provider, system provider, deployer: who you are

Most confusion about the AI Act comes from not knowing which role you play.

- If you **train a general-purpose model** and make it available in the EU, you are a **GPAI model provider** and the Article 53 duties (and Article 55, above the systemic-risk threshold) are yours.
- If you **build a product on someone else's model** (an API or open weights) and put it on the EU market under your name, you are the **provider of an AI system**. The model duties stay with the model provider; you are responsible for your system's classification (is it high-risk?), its transparency duties, and its documentation.
- If you **use an AI system in your own operations**, you are a **deployer**, with duties that depend on the risk class (for high-risk uses: human oversight, monitoring, logs).
- If you **significantly modify a general-purpose model**, for example by substantial fine-tuning, the Commission's guidelines say you may become the provider of the modified model, for the modification. Minor modifications do not trigger this.

For a typical team building on Claude, GPT, Gemini, or Grok through an API, the practical duties are: tell people when they are talking to an AI (Article 50(1)); make sure generated synthetic content is marked where the rules require it; check every use case against the high-risk list (screening job applicants or scoring creditworthiness puts you in a very different regime from summarizing meeting notes); support staff AI literacy; and collect the documentation your model provider must give downstream providers, because you will need it for your own.

## United States: federal preemption pressure, state frontier laws

**Federal policy.** The administration's AI Action Plan (July 2025) set a policy of accelerating AI development with minimal regulatory burden. **Executive Order 14365**, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" (11 December 2025), went further against state rules. It directed the Attorney General to create an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws inconsistent with the federal policy; told the Commerce Department to evaluate state laws and identify "onerous" ones; tied some federal broadband funding to states' AI laws; asked the FTC and FCC for policy actions; and called for a legislative recommendation for a federal framework that would preempt conflicting state laws, while preserving state authority in areas such as child safety. An executive order cannot by itself preempt state law, and as of November 2026 there is no federal AI statute of general application, so the state laws below are in force or scheduled.

**California SB 53**, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, signed on 29 September 2025 and in effect from 1 January 2026, applies to large frontier developers. It requires them to publish a framework on their websites describing how they incorporate national and international standards and industry best practices, creates a channel for reporting critical safety incidents to the state's Office of Emergency Services, protects whistleblowers, and is enforced by the Attorney General with civil penalties.

**New York's RAISE Act** was signed on 19 December 2025 and amended on 27 March 2026. As summarized by counsel, the amended law takes effect on **1 January 2027**, covers developers of models trained with more than 10 to the 26 operations that have over 500 million dollars in annual revenue, requires a published frontier AI framework and transparency reports, and requires reporting critical safety incidents within **72 hours** (24 hours when there is imminent risk of death or serious injury), with oversight by a new office in the Department of Financial Services.

The labs' safety frameworks from lesson 7 are, in part, how they comply with these laws; OpenAI's May 2026 Frontier Governance Framework explicitly maps its practices onto them.
</Listen>

<Callout type="claim" title="Claim: state AI laws create a patchwork that harms innovation and should be preempted (US administration, December 2025)">
**Who and when:** the White House, in [Executive Order 14365](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/eo-14365.pdf), 11 December 2025.

**Evidence for:** compliance with many different state regimes is costly, especially for smaller companies; the two leading state frontier laws already differ on reporting windows, penalties, and whistleblower protection (as counsel's comparison shows), which is the kind of divergence the order describes.

**Evidence against, or nuance:** the state frontier laws apply only to the largest developers, which have published frameworks anyway; New York amended its law in 2026 specifically to align it more closely with California's; and without a federal statute, preemption by litigation is slow and uncertain. Lab leaders themselves are divided: Amodei's January 2026 essay cited SB 53 and the RAISE Act as reasonable transparency measures.

**Score (November 2026):** a policy argument rather than a factual claim. The factual part (a patchwork exists) is true; whether it harms innovation more than it protects the public is contested.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Assuming the headline law is the one that applies to you">
Teams often spend weeks on frontier-model rules that bind only their vendor, while missing the ones that bind them: data protection law (GDPR, when personal data is in prompts, logs, or eval sets), sector rules for employment, credit, health, and consumer protection, the high-risk categories of the AI Act, and their own customers' contractual requirements. Start from your use case and your data, not from the news.
</Callout>

## What an engineering team should actually produce

Compliance work goes faster, and is more honest, when engineering supplies concrete artifacts. Five that serve almost every regime:

1. **An AI inventory.** Every feature that calls a model: purpose, users, model and version, provider, data sent, outputs, who reviews them. This is the input to every risk classification.
2. **Disclosures and marking.** User-facing notice that people are interacting with AI, and machine-readable marking of generated media where required, implemented and tested like any other feature.
3. **Logs and retention.** What you log, for how long, who can see it, and how it is deleted (Studio 6 covers the privacy side). High-risk regimes require logs; data protection limits them; design for both.
4. **Documentation from and for others.** The model provider's documentation and usage policies, filed with your system's own documentation: intended use, known limitations, eval results, and the model selection record from lesson 3.
5. **An incident process.** How a harmful output or misuse is detected, triaged, fixed, and, where the law requires, reported, with named owners. The labs now report incidents to regulators within days; your process should at least reach your own leadership within hours.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a useful voluntary structure for organizing these (govern, map, measure, manage), and procurement teams increasingly ask for it by name.
</Listen>

## What to carry forward

In the EU, general-purpose model duties have applied since August 2025 and became enforceable in August 2026; high-risk duties were pushed to late 2027 and 2028 by the 2026 Digital Omnibus; transparency duties apply now. Know whether you are a model provider, a system provider, or a deployer, because the duties follow the role. In the US, federal policy pushes toward preemption without a statute, while California's SB 53 is in force and New York's amended RAISE Act starts in January 2027, both aimed at the largest developers. For your team, the work is an inventory, disclosures, logs, documentation, and an incident process, built as engineering artifacts and checked like code.
</Listen>
