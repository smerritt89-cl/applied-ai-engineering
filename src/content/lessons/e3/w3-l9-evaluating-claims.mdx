## One method for every claim

This elective has examined claims about scaling, benchmarks, model quality, timelines, critics, work, safety, and law. They differ in subject, but you have been applying the same method each time. This lesson makes it explicit, gives you two quantitative tools to support it, works one example end to end, and then steps back to the whole program.

The method has six parts:

1. **Who says it, with what access?** A lab leader sees unreleased models; an academic sees published data; a journalist sees the speaker. Access is a reason to listen, not to believe.
2. **What are their incentives?** Fundraising, product launches, regulatory positioning, contrarian visibility, a competing approach. Incentives do not make a claim false; they set how much independent evidence you should require.
3. **What kind of evidence?** Randomized experiment, field study, benchmark, usage data, vendor-run eval, expert opinion, anecdote. Each answers a different question (lesson 6).
4. **Measured or extrapolated?** Which part was observed, on what population, and which part extends a trend beyond it (lesson 1)?
5. **What are the base rates?** How often have claims of this kind held up before?
6. **Has it been replicated, or can you test it yourself?** Independent replication, or a small eval on your own task (lesson 3).

Then write a verdict: one paragraph, ending with a confidence level and the evidence that would change it.
</Listen>

## Base rates: why "a study found" is not the end

In 2005, John Ioannidis argued in PLoS Medicine that most published research findings in many fields are false, and gave a simple model for why. The chance that a reported positive finding is true depends on three things: the **prior odds** that a tested hypothesis is true in that field, the study's **power** to detect a real effect, and **bias** (selective reporting, flexible analysis, many variants tried with one reported). With a prior of 1 in 10, 80% power, and the conventional 5% false-positive rate, a positive finding is true only about 64% of the time, before any bias.

AI is a field with all three risk factors. Many claimed improvements are small, so power is low unless the eval is large. Researchers and vendors try many prompts, scaffolds, and settings and report the best, which is bias in Ioannidis's sense; "The Leaderboard Illusion" (lesson 2) documented exactly this for arena submissions. And benchmarks leak into training, which is a form of leakage that Kapoor and Narayanan documented across hundreds of ML papers in 2022.

The practical consequence: discount single, unreplicated results, especially surprising ones and especially ones with a small effect. Then ask what independent work, or your own eval, would show.
</Listen>

## Scoring forecasters: the Brier score

Timeline claims are forecasts, and forecasts can be scored. The standard tool is the **Brier score**: for each forecast, take the stated probability p and the outcome o (1 if it happened, 0 if not), and average the squared error (p minus o) squared. Lower is better. A forecaster who always says 50% scores 0.25 on any set of yes-or-no events, so 0.25 is the no-skill baseline: a forecaster who scores worse than that would have done better by saying "I don't know" every time.

Two lessons follow. First, confidence is costly when wrong: a 90% forecast that fails contributes 0.81. Bold forecasters who are often wrong score badly even if they are spectacularly right sometimes. Second, you can only score forecasters who state probabilities on many checkable events. Most public AI claims are single, unhedged or vaguely hedged statements, which is why the previous lessons rewrote them before scoring. If someone wants to be taken seriously as a forecaster, the best evidence is a public, scored track record.
</Listen>

## A worked example: "agents will handle month-long projects within about a year"

Here is the method applied to a claim of a kind you will hear often in late 2026: that because AI task horizons double every few months, agents will reliably complete month-long software projects within about a year.

**Source.** The quantitative basis is METR's work: the March 2025 paper, the Time Horizon 1.1 update (29 January 2026), and the Frontier Risk Report (19 May 2026). The strongest lab-leader version is Amodei's September 2026 essay, which argues progress sped up from mid-2026. Note that METR itself does not make the one-year claim; it is an extrapolation others draw from METR's data.

**Rewrite so it can fail.** "By 30 November 2027, an independent evaluator reports a frontier agent with an 80% time horizon of at least 167 hours (one working month) on software tasks."

**Access and incentives.** METR has privileged access to labs and states that it took no payment for its 2026 incident investigation; lab leaders have the most access and strong incentives toward short timelines.

**Evidence type; measured versus extrapolated.** Measured: on METR's suite, 50% horizons doubled about every 131 days since 2023; in early 2026 the public frontier was about 12 hours at 50% and about 1.5 hours at 80%. Extrapolated: everything after that. The 80% figure, not the 50% one, is what "reliably" means.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## The worked example, continued

**Base rates and caveats.** The arithmetic from lesson 4: getting from 1.5 hours to 167 hours at 80% is about 6.8 doublings, roughly 2.4 years at a 131-day doubling time or 1.7 years at the faster 89-day estimate, so "about a year" requires faster-than-measured growth at the reliability that matters. METR states its suite is unreliable above about 16 hours and stopped updating its public page in September 2026, so the measurement that would confirm the claim does not currently exist. It also found at least 16% of long-task "successes" were cheating. Software tasks are where models improved fastest, so transfer to other work is a further extrapolation.

**Replication.** METR's May 2026 report found public models above 100 hours on an early version of a narrower benchmark (MirrorCode), which saturated; that supports fast progress, but on cleaner tasks.

**Verdict.** The direction is well supported; the one-year timing at 80% reliability on messy software work is not, and will be hard to check until new long-task benchmarks exist. Confidence that the rewritten claim resolves "yes": low to moderate, perhaps 20 to 35%. Evidence that would raise it: an independent long-task suite with human baselines showing 80% horizons above a week by mid-2027.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Evaluating the claim you expected instead of the one that was made">
The commonest error in claim evaluation is to argue with a paraphrase. Headlines compress "could be as little as one to two years away, though it could take considerably longer" into "AGI in two years," and then both believers and skeptics respond to the headline. Before you analyze anything, quote the primary source and note the hedges. If you cannot find the primary source, say so in your verdict and lower your confidence; secondhand claims are a different, weaker kind of evidence.
</Callout>

## The checks and balances of the whole program

Step back from this elective to the whole program. Every studio taught a version of the same idea: model output is a claim, and claims need checks whose failures are independent of the thing being checked.

- **Studio 1** validated structured output in code, required evidence quotes and verified them as exact substrings, and routed doubtful items to people. A schema is not a fact-check.
- **Studio 2** measured retrieval separately from generation and required citations that point to retrieved text, because a fluent answer can be faithful to the wrong passage.
- **Studio 3** built evals with held-out splits, intervals, and paired comparisons, and calibrated LLM judges against human labels, because a judge is another model output.
- **Studio 4** gated agent actions with permissions, sandboxes, and human approval for anything irreversible, and treated tool results as untrusted input.
- **Studio 5** checked fine-tuning data for contamination and compared against a frontier baseline on held-out data.
- **Studio 6** made systems observable and planned incident response, because checks that nobody watches are not checks.
- **This elective** applied the same logic to claims about AI itself: primary sources, dated; measured versus extrapolated; base rates; independent replication; your own small eval.

The unifying principle is **independence of checks**. A model grading its own work, a vendor reporting its own benchmark, a lab grading its own safety framework, an analysis agent summarizing transcripts from its own model family: each is a check whose failures are correlated with the thing it checks. METR's investigators were explicit about this when they relied on GPT-5.6 Sol to analyze agents that included GPT-5.6 Sol. The remedy is always the same: add a check that fails differently (deterministic code, a person, a different model family, an independent organization), sized to the stakes.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Becoming a better forecaster yourself">
If you want to improve your own judgment about AI claims, the most effective practice is to make probabilistic predictions about checkable events, write them down with dates, and score yourself with the Brier score when they resolve. Start with questions close to your work: "Will our eval show model X beating model Y on extraction by more than 5 points?" or "Will provider Z cut prices on its small tier this quarter?" Over a year, you will learn whether your 80% means 80%. Chip Huyen's AI Engineering makes a related point about evaluation in general: the discipline is less about any single metric than about building the habit of checking.
</GoDeeper>
