## From an engine to a learner

Last lesson you built the engine: a `Value` that records operations and backpropagates. This lesson, covering the second half of the lecture, turns it into something that learns. The steps are few. A neuron is a weighted sum plus a bias, passed through a nonlinearity. A layer is a list of neurons; a multilayer perceptron (MLP) is a list of layers. A loss function measures how wrong the network's predictions are, as one number. Backpropagation gives the gradient of that number with respect to every weight. Gradient descent nudges every weight a little against its gradient. Repeat.

Nothing in that list needs new calculus. A neural network, for this lecture's purposes, is just a big expression, and you already know how to differentiate big expressions. The new ideas are about what you differentiate (the loss), and what you do with the gradient (the update).
</Listen>

## Granularity doesn't matter, correctness does

At 01:27:05 the video replaces the single tanh node with its definition, tanh(x) = (e to the 2x, minus 1) divided by (e to the 2x, plus 1), built from exp, addition, power, and multiplication nodes. The gradient comes out identical. This is the chain rule doing its job: the derivative of a composition doesn't depend on how you group the pieces.

This has two practical consequences. First, you're free to choose the granularity of your operations. Frameworks fuse common compositions (tanh, softmax plus cross-entropy, layer norm) into single operations with hand-derived backward passes, because the fused version is faster and often more numerically stable. Second, any operation you can write as a composition of differentiable primitives gets a correct backward pass automatically. You only need to hand-derive a backward pass when you want a fused, faster, or more stable version, and then you owe it a gradient check.

Division is a nice example. micrograd implements a divided by b as a times b to the power minus 1, so the only new local derivative is the power rule: d(x to the k)/dx = k x to the (k minus 1).
</Listen>

## Neurons, layers, and parameters

A neuron with inputs x1 through xn computes tanh(w1 x1 + ... + wn xn + b). Its weights and bias are its **parameters**, the numbers training adjusts. A layer of m such neurons maps a vector in R^n to a vector in R^m. Stack layers and you have an MLP: `MLP(3, [4, 4, 1])` maps 3 inputs through two hidden layers of 4 neurons to a single output.

Mathematically, a layer is x maps to tanh(W x + b), with W an m-by-n matrix applied elementwise through tanh. The scalar version in micrograd builds that matrix product out of individual multiply and add nodes, which is why it is slow and why it is so good for learning: you can inspect every single number.

Why the nonlinearity? Without it, a stack of layers collapses to a single affine map (a product of matrices is a matrix), so depth would add nothing. With it, the MLP can approximate a much richer family of functions. And tanh in particular squashes outputs into the interval from minus 1 to 1, which matches the targets of plus or minus 1 in the video's tiny dataset.
</Listen>

## A loss turns "good" into a number

Training needs a single scalar to minimize. The video uses the sum of squared errors over the dataset: L = sum over examples of (prediction minus target) squared. Its derivative with respect to a prediction is 2 (prediction minus target), so each example pushes its own prediction toward its target with a force proportional to the error. Big mistakes get big gradients.

The choice of loss is a modeling decision, not a detail. Squared error is the natural loss for regression with Gaussian noise (minimizing it is maximum likelihood under that assumption). From the next lesson on, the series switches to **negative log-likelihood** (cross-entropy), the natural loss for predicting a category such as the next character. The training machinery doesn't change at all: one scalar, one backward pass, one update.
</Listen>

```text
L(theta)        = sum_i (yhat_i(theta) - y_i)^2
dL/dyhat_i      = 2 (yhat_i - y_i)

update:         theta  <-  theta - eta * grad L(theta)

why it works:   L(theta - eta g) = L(theta) - eta ||g||^2 + O(eta^2)
                so for small enough eta > 0 the loss decreases whenever g != 0
```

## Gradient descent, and why the step size matters

The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. A first-order Taylor expansion makes the update rule precise: moving the parameters by minus eta times the gradient g changes the loss by about minus eta times the squared norm of g, which is negative whenever g isn't zero. So small steps against the gradient always help, at least locally.

"Small enough" is doing real work in that sentence. The second-order term grows with eta squared and with the curvature of the loss. Too small a learning rate and training crawls; too large and the quadratic term dominates, so steps overshoot and the loss oscillates or diverges. Lesson 4 shows a practical way to find a good learning rate. Optimizers such as Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014), whose AdamW variant the "Let's build GPT" lecture code uses, adapt the step size per parameter, but they are still built on the same gradient.

Notice also how forgiving this is. Any direction with a positive inner product with the true gradient decreases the loss for small steps. A buggy gradient that is merely "roughly right" will still train, just badly. That's why a falling loss is weak evidence that your backward pass is correct, and why the gradient check from lesson 1 stays in your toolkit.
</Listen>

## How this scales to real networks

The lecture's summary at 02:14:03 makes the point that everything you just did is what large networks do, with three differences of scale rather than kind. Values become tensors, so one node is a whole matrix multiply with a hand-written, vectorized backward. The dataset is too large to use in every step, so each step estimates the gradient from a random minibatch (lesson 4). And the loss is cross-entropy over a vocabulary rather than squared error on one output.

This is a useful lens for reading about large models as an applied engineer. When a model card describes pretraining, fine-tuning, or preference tuning, each one is "pick a loss, compute its gradient on batches of data, step the parameters." What changes between them is the data and the loss. If you took Studio 5, you saw supervised fine-tuning and LoRA from the outside; this is the inside.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="When does gradient descent provably decrease the loss?">
If the gradient of L is Lipschitz with constant K (L is "K-smooth"), then for any step size eta at most 1/K, the descent lemma gives L(theta minus eta g) at most L(theta) minus (eta/2) times the squared norm of g. So each step decreases the loss by an amount proportional to the squared gradient norm, and the gradient norm must go to zero over many steps. For convex L you also get convergence to the minimum; neural network losses aren't convex, so the guarantee is only to a stationary point. In practice, the curvature constant isn't known, which is why learning rates are found empirically and why schedules (warmup, decay) exist. Chapter 4.3 and chapter 8 of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville's *Deep Learning* cover gradient-based optimization and the specific difficulties of training deep networks: ill-conditioning, saddle points, and cliffs.
</GoDeeper>

## Review sheet

Any decomposition of a function backpropagates correctly if each local derivative is right; frameworks fuse operations for speed and stability, not correctness. A neuron is tanh of an affine map, a layer is many neurons, an MLP is layers in sequence; a layer with n inputs and m outputs has m(n + 1) parameters. A loss reduces "how wrong" to one number; squared error's gradient is twice the error. Gradient descent steps against the gradient; the Taylor expansion says small steps help, and the learning rate decides how small. Zero the gradients before every backward pass.
