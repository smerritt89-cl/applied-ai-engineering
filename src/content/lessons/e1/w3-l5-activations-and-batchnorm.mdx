## Where this lesson sits

Week 3 covers two of the three remaining makemore lectures: this one on activations, gradients, and BatchNorm, and next lesson's "Backprop Ninja." Part 5 (WaveNet) is optional and appears in the GoDeeper at the end. These two were chosen because they explain the things you'll see in every transformer: why layers are initialized the way they are, why there's a normalization layer in every block, and how gradients actually flow through tensor operations.

The lecture's theme is that a deep network is a long product of operations, and products are fragile. If each layer slightly grows the scale of its activations, the growth compounds; if each slightly shrinks the gradient, the gradient vanishes by the time it reaches the first layer. The fixes, careful initialization and normalization layers, are best understood as controlling variances, which is calculation you can do on paper.

## The loss you should see at step zero

Before any training, a classifier knows nothing, so it should spread probability roughly evenly. For 27 characters, that means an initial loss near log 27, about 3.30. The lecture's network starts far above that, because its output layer produces large logits at random: the softmax is confidently wrong, and the loss pays for confidence it hasn't earned. The first many training steps are then spent just shrinking the logits, the "hockey stick" at the start of the loss curve.

The fix at 04:19 is to initialize the output layer's weights small and its bias at zero, so the initial logits are near 0 and the softmax is near uniform. The general rule is worth memorizing as a check: **compute the loss you expect at initialization and assert you see it.** For a C-way classifier that is log C. For a model whose initial loss is far above that baseline, something is scaled wrong, and the first part of training will be wasted undoing it.

This check transfers beyond toy models. When you fine-tune, the initial loss on your data tells you whether the data and the model agree before you've changed anything. A suspicious starting value is often a tokenization or formatting bug, not a modeling problem.
</Listen>

## Saturated tanh, and gradients that vanish

The hidden layer is tanh of a pre-activation. If the pre-activations are large, most tanh outputs sit at plus or minus 1. The derivative of tanh is 1 minus t squared, so at t = 0.99 the unit passes back only about 2 percent of the gradient it receives. A unit that is saturated for every example in the batch passes back essentially nothing: its incoming weights stop learning. The lecture's white-pixel plot (12:59) shows units against examples, white wherever the output is very close to plus or minus 1, and a column of all white is a dead neuron.

Other nonlinearities fail differently. ReLU has derivative exactly 0 for negative inputs, so a ReLU unit whose pre-activation is negative for all inputs is permanently dead (a large learning rate can knock units into that state). Sigmoid saturates at both ends like tanh. Every squashing nonlinearity has a flat region, and the question is how much of the data lands there.

The fix is again a matter of scale: initialize the first layer so pre-activations have a standard deviation around 1, not 10. Which raises the question: what scale is right, in general?
</Listen>

```text
y = sum_{i=1..n} w_i x_i          w_i, x_i independent, zero mean

Var(y) = sum_i Var(w_i x_i) = n Var(w) Var(x)

keep Var(y) = Var(x)   =>   Var(w) = 1 / n   =>   std(w) = gain / sqrt(fan_in)

gain: 1 for linear, sqrt(2) for ReLU (He et al. 2015), 5/3 for tanh (PyTorch calculate_gain)
```

## Kaiming initialization, derived

Take one output of a linear layer: y is the sum over n inputs of w_i times x_i, with weights and inputs independent and zero-mean. Variances of independent terms add, and the variance of a product of independent zero-mean variables is the product of variances. So Var(y) = n Var(w) Var(x). To keep the scale constant from layer to layer, set Var(w) = 1/n, a weight standard deviation of 1 over the square root of fan_in.

Nonlinearities change the picture by a constant factor. ReLU zeroes half of a symmetric input, halving the second moment, so He and colleagues (2015) derived a correction: weight variance 2/n, a gain of sqrt(2). tanh is contractive (its slope is at most 1), so it shrinks the variance a little, and PyTorch's `calculate_gain` uses 5/3 to compensate. Glorot and Bengio (2010) earlier made the same argument for the backward pass and proposed averaging fan_in and fan_out so both directions are roughly preserved.

The lecture's point at 27:53 is that you can see this empirically: draw a batch, push it through a layer with different weight scales, and look at the standard deviation coming out. The exercise below does exactly that. It also notes the limits. These derivations assume independence and specific distributions, and they become less critical once normalization layers and better optimizers are in place, which is part of why modern networks train at all at depth.
</Listen>

## Batch Normalization

Ioffe and Szegedy (2015) proposed a blunter instrument: if you want pre-activations to be roughly standard normal, standardize them. For each feature, compute the mean and variance over the current minibatch, subtract and divide, and then apply a learned scale gamma and shift beta, so the network can undo the normalization wherever that helps. The layer sits between a linear layer and its nonlinearity.

Several consequences follow, and the lecture covers each:

- **Redundant bias.** Mean subtraction removes any constant the previous layer added, so its bias does nothing and is usually disabled. Beta takes over the job. You'll see this as `bias=False` before BatchNorm in the ResNet code the lecture reads at 01:04:50.
- **Two modes.** At inference there may be one example, so batch statistics don't exist. BatchNorm keeps running averages of the mean and variance during training (PyTorch's default momentum is 0.1) and uses them at inference. Forgetting to switch modes is a classic bug.
- **Batch coupling.** During training each example's output depends on the others in its batch. That jitter acts as a regularizer, but it also makes behavior depend on batch size and composition. The lecture's description links Wu and Johnson's "Rethinking Batch in BatchNorm" (2021) for the practical problems this causes.

A detail you'll meet next lesson: PyTorch's `BatchNorm1d` uses the biased variance (divide by n) to normalize during training, but the unbiased estimate (divide by n minus 1) for the running variance. The Backprop Ninja lecture digresses on exactly this mismatch.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Train mode, eval mode, and batch size one">
The BatchNorm bugs that cost real teams days all come from its two modes. Evaluating in training mode makes predictions depend on what else is in the batch, so a model looks fine in batched evaluation and behaves differently when served one request at a time. Training with very small batches makes the batch statistics noisy. And forgetting to calibrate the running statistics (for example, after changing data) leaves inference normalizing with stale numbers. When a model's offline metrics and its served behavior disagree, normalization modes are one of the first things to check.
</Callout>

## Diagnostics: is the network healthy?

The second half of the lecture builds the habit of looking inside. Four plots, recomputed as training runs:

1. **Forward activations** per layer: their distribution and the fraction near saturation. Healthy layers have similar spreads; a layer piled up at the extremes is saturating.
2. **Backward gradients** per layer: their distribution should be similar across layers. Shrinking toward the input means vanishing gradients; growing means exploding.
3. **Parameter gradients**, especially the ratio of gradient spread to parameter spread for each weight matrix. One layer with a wildly different ratio (the lecture finds one) will train at a different speed from the rest.
4. **Update-to-data ratio** over time: for each parameter tensor, the standard deviation of the update (learning rate times gradient) divided by the standard deviation of the parameter, on a log scale. It says how fast each tensor is actually changing. Watch the lecture's discussion at 01:39:55 for the rough target he uses and what it means when a layer sits far above or below it.

The fully linear experiment at 01:32:07 is a nice piece of math made visible: without nonlinearities the whole network collapses to one linear map, yet the training dynamics still depend on initialization scale, because the gradients do.
</Listen>

## What a working engineer takes from this

You'll rarely hand-tune initializations in applied work; frameworks and pretrained checkpoints handle it. What transfers is the diagnostic stance. When a fine-tuning run diverges, plateaus, or produces NaNs, the questions from this lecture are the ones to ask, in order. Was the initial loss what you'd expect? Are any layers saturated or dead? Are gradients vanishing or exploding (gradient-norm logging catches this)? Is the update size reasonable relative to the weights, or is the learning rate wrong for this model?

That last question connects to model selection, too. Learning rates and schedules that work for one model size don't transfer automatically to another, so when you compare two fine-tuned models, make sure each had a fair hyperparameter search. Otherwise you're comparing tuning effort, not models, which is the same fairness rule Studio 3 applied to comparing prompts.
</Listen>
