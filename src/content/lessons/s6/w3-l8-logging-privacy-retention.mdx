## Logs are where privacy problems collect

In the last lesson you decided what to record on every model call. This lesson is about the other side of that decision: what you must not record, what you record only in protected form, and how long anything stays.

LLM systems make this harder than ordinary web services in a specific way. In a typical service, the sensitive data sits in a few known fields (a password, a card number). In an LLM system, the **payload is free text written by users**, and it flows everywhere: into prompts, into retrieved documents that get pasted into prompts, into tool arguments the model writes, and back out in the model's answer, which can quote the user or a document. A customer who types their phone number into a support chat has put personal data into your prompt, your trace, your log, your eval set, and possibly your review queue, all in one request.

So the question is not only "which fields are sensitive" but "which **copies** of user text exist, who can read each one, and when does each one die." Every copy you make is a copy you must protect, disclose when asked, and delete on request. The cheapest copy to protect is the one you never made.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## What not to log

The OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet is the standard starting list. It says these should usually not be recorded directly in logs: session identifiers (log a hash if you need to correlate), access tokens, passwords, database connection strings, encryption keys and other primary secrets, bank account and payment card data, sensitive personal data such as health data and government identifiers, data classified higher than the logging system is allowed to hold, commercially sensitive information, information that is illegal to collect where you operate, and information a user has opted out of or not consented to. It also flags ordinary personal data (names, phone numbers, email addresses) as needing special treatment: remove, mask, hash, or pseudonymize it when the person's identity isn't needed for the log's purpose.

For an LLM system, add these to the list:

- **Prompt and response text by default.** Record ids, versions, token counts, and outcomes. Capture content only under a policy that says where it goes, who can read it, and when it is deleted (the opt-in default from lesson 7).
- **Tool arguments and results.** The model writes tool arguments, and users can talk it into putting anything there. A `search_orders` call may carry an email address; a code tool's result may contain an environment variable.
- **Retrieved documents.** Log document ids and chunk ids, not the text. The text is already in your document store, under that store's access rules.
- **Secrets that reach the model.** If a key or token ever appears in a prompt (a user pastes one, a tool returns one), it is now in every copy of that prompt.
- **System prompts in client-visible logs.** Your prompts are intellectual property and sometimes contain internal rules you don't want exposed.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## Three ways to protect a value you keep

When a field is needed but sensitive, there are three standard treatments, from strongest to weakest.

**Don't collect it.** If the analysis doesn't need the user's email, the log doesn't get it. This is the data minimisation principle in GDPR Article 5(1)(c): personal data must be limited to what is necessary for the purpose.

**Pseudonymize it.** Replace the identifier with a stable token, so you can still group a user's records ("this user hit the error five times") without storing who they are. Use a **keyed** hash such as HMAC-SHA256, with the key held in your secrets manager. A plain hash of an email is reversible in practice: an attacker hashes a list of known emails and looks for matches. Be clear about what this buys you legally: GDPR Article 4(5) defines pseudonymisation as processing that needs separately held additional information to re-identify someone, and recital 26 says pseudonymised data that can be attributed to a person with that information is still personal data. Pseudonymization reduces risk and exposure; it doesn't make the log anonymous.

**Redact it.** Find sensitive spans inside free text and replace them with a placeholder such as `[EMAIL]`. This is the only option for content you've decided to keep, and it is the least reliable of the three, because it depends on recognizing the sensitive data. That's the exercise below.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Redacting the log but not the trace">
Teams often add a redaction step to the logger and forget every other exit: the tracing exporter with content capture on, the exception handler that logs the full request on failure, the eval pipeline that copies production traces into a dataset, and the debug print someone added during an incident. Redact at the point where content leaves the request handler, in one shared function that every exit uses, and test the exits, not just the function. In the lab, the same `redact` runs for every content preview, and a test checks that no raw email appears anywhere in the records.
</Callout>

## What your redactor misses

When the tests pass, list what still gets through. These are **false negatives**: sensitive data the redactor didn't catch.

- **Names, addresses, and free-text details.** "This is Maria Lopez from the Portland office" has no pattern for a regex to find. Catching it needs named-entity recognition, and even then accuracy varies with language and domain.
- **Obfuscated or reformatted values.** "ana at example dot com", a phone number split across a line break, digits written as words, or text in a non-Latin script.
- **Formats you didn't write.** The phone pattern above knows North American and E.164 formats. A UK number written as 020 7946 0958 sails through, as do extensions and many national formats.
- **Secrets without a recognizable prefix.** Your pattern catches `sk-` keys (Anthropic's admin keys, for instance, start with `sk-ant-admin`, per its Admin API docs) and bearer headers. A database password in a connection string, a random 32-character token, or a key inside a base64 blob gets through unless you add a rule for it.
- **Sensitive meaning without sensitive tokens.** "I was just diagnosed and can't work this month" contains health information and no pattern at all.

There are **false positives** too: a 10-digit order number matches the phone pattern, and over-eager rules turn logs into strings of placeholders that no one can debug from.

Better tools exist. Presidio, an open-source toolkit for finding and anonymizing PII (originally from Microsoft, now community-maintained), combines named-entity models with regular expressions, rule-based logic, checksums, and surrounding context, in several languages. Its own documentation says there is no guarantee it will find all sensitive information and recommends additional safeguards. That is the honest summary for any redactor, model-based ones included: redaction lowers the amount of sensitive data in your logs; it does not bring it to zero. You can measure it, though, the same way you measured judges in Studio 3: label a few hundred real (access-controlled) log lines by hand, run the redactor, and report recall per entity type. Then decide whether the residual risk is acceptable for each destination.
</Listen>

## What the providers keep, as published today

Your provider is one of the systems that holds copies of your prompts. These summaries come from each provider's published policy pages, read on the review date. Policies change and contracts override defaults, so confirm against the current page and your own agreement before relying on any of it.

**Anthropic (Claude API, commercial).** Its privacy center says API inputs and outputs are deleted within 30 days of receipt or generation by default, with exceptions: content flagged by trust and safety systems may be kept up to 2 years, data tied to feedback you submit is kept for 5 years, and services you control (such as files you upload) follow their own lifetimes. Its retention docs say retained data is never used for model training without your express permission. **Zero data retention (ZDR)** is an arrangement you request through Anthropic's sales team; it is enabled per organization, and it covers only features marked eligible in a published table. Stateful features (for example the Files API, code execution containers, and the MCP connector) are not ZDR-eligible, and some models, which Anthropic calls Covered Models, require 30-day retention and are not available under ZDR at all. Even under ZDR, Anthropic may retain flagged content where required by law or for safety. For health data there is a separate **HIPAA readiness** arrangement with a signed business associate agreement (BAA), which blocks non-eligible features with an error.

**OpenAI (API).** Its data controls guide says API data is not used to train models unless you opt in. Abuse-monitoring logs are kept for up to 30 days by default. Zero Data Retention and Modified Abuse Monitoring both require OpenAI's approval. Application state is a separate question: the Responses API stores response data for at least 30 days by default (or when `store` is true), and several endpoints, including files, assistants, threads, vector stores, batches, and fine-tuning, are not ZDR-eligible.

**Google (Gemini API and Vertex AI).** The Gemini API terms distinguish **Unpaid Services** (free AI Studio use and unpaid API quota), where Google uses submitted content and responses to improve its products and human reviewers may read them, and the terms tell you not to submit sensitive, confidential, or personal information; from **Paid Services**, where Google doesn't use prompts or responses to improve products but logs them for a limited period for abuse detection and legal requirements. Apps serving users in the EEA, Switzerland, or the UK must use Paid Services. On **Vertex AI**, Google's docs say it won't train on your data without permission, but zero retention takes configuration: in-memory caching is on by default (data deleted within 24 hours) and can be disabled, request-response logging must stay off, and some features, such as Grounding with Google Search, store data for days in a way you can't turn off.

The pattern across all three: no training on paid API traffic by default, a default retention window for abuse monitoring, ZDR as an approved arrangement rather than a switch, and feature-level exceptions that break ZDR quietly. When a privacy review asks "does our provider keep this?", the right answer names the arrangement, the features in use, and the date you checked.

## Who can read the logs, and for how long

Treat logs and traces that contain user content as production data, because they are. The OWASP cheat sheet's guidance on protecting logs applies directly: restrict who can read them, review those privileges periodically, record and monitor access to the logs themselves, protect them against tampering, use secure transport when they cross networks, and keep them for the required period and no longer.

A structure that works for LLM systems is **two tiers**:

- **Metadata tier**: ids, versions, token counts, latency, cost, status, pseudonymous user ids, redaction counts. Broadly readable by the engineering team, retained long enough for trend analysis (often months). This is what your dashboards query.
- **Content tier**: redacted prompts and outputs, captured only where policy allows, in a separate store with its own access list (named reviewers, on-call during an incident), short retention (days to weeks), and access logging. Spans and metadata records point to it by id. This is the external-storage pattern from the semantic conventions.

Retention needs an owner and a mechanism. Set time-to-live on every store at creation, not "later." Make deletion requests reach every tier: if a customer exercises the right to erasure under GDPR (Article 17), the request has to find their pseudonymous records, their content in the content tier, and any copies in eval datasets. Keeping the HMAC key lets you compute the pseudonym for a known user to find their rows; deleting the key is one way to make old pseudonyms unlinkable, if your policy chooses that.
</Listen>

## Compliance basics, described accurately

This is orientation, not legal advice. The point is to know which questions to bring to your privacy or legal colleagues, early.

**GDPR** (the EU's General Data Protection Regulation) applies to personal data of people in the EU, and its Article 5 principles map directly onto logging decisions: **purpose limitation** (collect for specified purposes and don't repurpose incompatibly; "debugging" and "training an internal model on customer chats" are different purposes), **data minimisation** (only what's necessary), and **storage limitation** (keep identifiable data no longer than necessary). Pseudonymized logs are still in scope, as above. Other jurisdictions have their own laws with different details.

**Health data in the US** brings in HIPAA. You can see the shape of the requirement in how providers package it: Anthropic offers HIPAA-ready API access only with a signed business associate agreement (BAA) and only for eligible features. Any other vendor that could receive protected health information through your system, your observability vendor included, needs the same review with your compliance team.

**Contracts and customer commitments** often matter more day to day than statutes: enterprise customers commonly specify where data may be processed, how long it may be kept, and which subprocessors may see it. Your tracing vendor is a subprocessor.

The engineer's job is not to interpret the law. It's to make the data flows legible (which copies exist, where, for how long, who can read them), to default to collecting less, and to raise the question before launch rather than after an incident. A one-page data-flow diagram of your LLM system, with every store and its retention, is the most useful thing you can bring to that conversation.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Prompt logging and model selection">
Data handling is a model-selection criterion, not an afterthought. Two systems with equal quality differ if one supports the zero-retention arrangement your customer contract requires and the other only offers it for some features. Before choosing a provider or feature for a sensitive workload, check four things against current documentation: whether the feature you need is eligible for the arrangement you need (a hosted tool or file store can break ZDR on its own), where inference and storage happen (several providers now offer region controls), what the abuse-monitoring exception allows, and whether the free tier you prototyped on has different terms from the paid tier you'll ship on (the Gemini API is an explicit example). Open-weight models you host yourself remove the provider copy entirely, and move the whole retention and security burden to you.
</GoDeeper>

## The rules you'll apply in the lab

Collect less: ids and numbers by default, content only by policy. Pseudonymize users with a keyed hash. Redact every captured value in one shared function, count what it redacts, and test the exits as well as the function. Know its false negatives, and protect the content tier with access control and short retention because the redactor will miss things. Know what your provider keeps, what your arrangement covers, and the date you checked. The lab's records follow these rules, and the validator rejects a record whose preview still contains something the redactor would catch.
</Listen>
