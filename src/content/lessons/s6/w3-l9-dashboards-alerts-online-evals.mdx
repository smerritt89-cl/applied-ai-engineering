## From traces to a picture of the system

Traces and logs answer questions about individual requests. A dashboard answers a different question: **is the system healthy right now, and is it getting better or worse?** Alerts answer a narrower one: **does a person need to act now?** Online evaluation answers the question that ordinary monitoring can't: **are the answers still good?**

Google's SRE book offers the classic starting point for any user-facing service: if you can measure only four things, measure **latency, traffic, errors, and saturation**. All four apply to LLM systems, with translations. Latency includes time to first token for streamed answers. Errors include failures that return HTTP 200, such as refusals and truncations. Saturation includes your provider's rate limits, which you hit long before your own servers are busy. LLM systems then add two signals that ordinary services don't have: **cost**, which scales with every token, and **quality**, which can collapse while every other signal is green.
</Listen>

## The metrics that matter

**Cost.** Chart cost per request and per completed task, broken down by feature, model, and prompt version, plus total daily spend against a budget. Cost per completed task is the one that guides decisions: a cheaper model that needs two retries or a human fix isn't cheaper. Compute cost from token counts and a versioned price table, and reconcile weekly against the provider's own usage reporting: Anthropic's docs note that exported spans are delivered on a best-effort basis, so a sum over spans can undercount.

**Latency percentiles.** Use p50, p95, and p99, not the mean. The SRE book's point is that an average hides a slow tail; record latency in histogram buckets so you can compute any percentile later. Chart time to first token separately for streamed responses, and track latency of failed requests separately too: the book notes that a slow error is even worse than a fast one, and timeouts are slow errors by definition.

**Error rates, by type.** Split them, because each type has a different owner and fix. Rate limiting (HTTP 429 on Claude) means you need more capacity or better backoff. Provider overload (529, `overloaded_error`, on Claude) means retries with jitter and possibly a fallback model. Timeouts may mean `max_tokens` is set too high for a synchronous path. Your own validation failures (a structured output that parses but fails your checks) point at the prompt.

**Refusals and truncations.** These look like success at the HTTP layer. Claude reports `stop_reason: "refusal"` (with a category in `stop_details`) and `max_tokens` when output was cut off; other providers have equivalents, such as the refusal field in OpenAI's structured outputs. Track each as a rate per route. A refusal rate that doubles after a prompt change is a regression even though nothing errored.

**Quality signals.** The share of sampled answers that pass your graders (next section), user feedback rates, and task-level outcomes where you have them (a ticket resolved without escalation, a drafted reply sent without edits). Tool-level signals matter for agents: tool error rate, calls per task, and loop length.

Break every metric down by the dimensions you recorded in lesson 7: model, prompt version, route, and customer tier. A global average can stay flat while one prompt version on one route falls apart.
</Listen>

## Online evaluation on sampled traffic

Offline evals (Studio 3) tell you whether a change is safe to ship. **Online evals** tell you whether the shipped system is still good on the traffic it actually gets. The mechanics reuse what you already built.

**Sample.** Take a uniform random sample of production traces for measurement, as Studio 3's review-queue lesson showed. Keep it separate from targeted samples (thumbs-down, judge failures, latency outliers), which are for discovery and are deliberately enriched with failures. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's evals FAQ makes the same split. Run the graders asynchronously, off the request path, so evaluation never adds latency or failure modes to the user's request.

**Grade.** Run the cheap, certain graders on every sampled trace: code-based checks such as citation verification, schema rules, and forbidden-content filters. Run the LLM judge from Studio 3, with the prompt you calibrated against human labels, on the same sample. Several tracing tools (Langfuse and LangSmith among them) can run evaluators on production traces for you; the statistics are the same either way.

**Size the sample.** You have the math. To estimate a pass rate near 0.9 within about plus or minus 3 points at 95 percent confidence, the normal approximation gives n of about 1.96 squared times 0.9 times 0.1, divided by 0.03 squared, which is about 384 judged traces per reporting window. At 20,000 requests a day, a 2 percent sample gives that daily. At 2,000 a day, report weekly instead of sampling more aggressively than your judge budget allows. The judge has a cost too: price it with the same table as everything else.

**Correct.** A judge's raw pass rate is biased by its own errors. Studio 3 showed the Rogan and Gladen correction: with the judge's true positive rate (TPR) and true negative rate (TNR) measured on human labels, the estimated true pass rate is (observed + TNR - 1) / (TPR + TNR - 1). Chart the corrected rate with its interval, and keep a person relabeling a small random sample every week or two, because a judge's TPR and TNR drift when traffic does.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Grading your own outputs with the same blind spots">
If production answers come from one model and the online judge is the same model with a different prompt, the judge can share the generator's failure modes, and Studio 3 covered the evidence for self-preference in LLM judges. Calibration against human labels is what tells you how much that matters on your task. When stakes are high, consider a judge from a different model family, measured the same way, rather than assuming either is neutral.
</Callout>

## Drift: when the world moves under a working system

Chip Huyen's essay on distribution shifts gives the classic vocabulary from ML: **covariate shift** (the input distribution changes), **label shift** (the output distribution changes), and **concept drift** (the right answer for the same input changes). It also makes a practical point: you rarely get ground-truth labels quickly in production, so you monitor what you can see without labels, the inputs and the model's outputs, as early warning. Her essay also cites a Google review in which most of the failures examined came from ordinary software causes rather than ML-specific ones, a useful reminder to check deploys and dependencies first.

For LLM systems, watch four kinds:

- **Input drift.** Topic mix, language, input length, share of inputs with attachments. A new customer segment or a marketing campaign changes what users ask.
- **Output drift.** Answer length, refusal rate, tool calls per task, share of answers citing no source. These move when the model or prompt behaves differently, even before anyone labels a failure.
- **Model drift.** Providers update models and aliases. Record the response's model identifier on every call, so a change in behavior can be lined up against a change in the model actually serving you.
- **Judge drift.** Your evaluator is a model too. If the corrected pass rate jumps the day you upgraded the judge's model, that is the judge changing, not your product.

Compare each metric against a baseline window (last month, or the week before a release) and look for changes that persist across several windows, not single-day blips. Drift is almost always ticket work: something to investigate this week, not wake someone for tonight.
</Listen>

## User feedback: useful, biased, never the whole story

Explicit feedback (thumbs up and down, ratings, a "report" button) is cheap to collect and valuable for finding failures, but it's a biased sample: most users never click, and the ones who do skew toward strong reactions. Implicit signals often tell you more: the user regenerates the answer, edits a drafted reply heavily before sending, copies the answer, abandons the session, or escalates to a human.

Use feedback two ways, and keep them apart. As **discovery**, every thumbs-down goes into the review queue with its trace id attached, which is why the trace id belongs on every feedback event. As **measurement**, chart feedback rates over time per route and version, but never treat the thumbs-down rate as your quality estimate: the random sample with a calibrated judge is your measurement, and feedback is a second, biased signal that should move in the same direction. When the two disagree, investigate; that disagreement is information.
</Listen>

## Alerts that wake people only for good reasons

The SRE book's rules for what should page are short: a page must be urgent, actionable, and about a symptom users feel, not a cause, and every page should be actionable. The SRE workbook adds four properties to judge any alert by: **precision** (the share of alerts that were real), **recall** (the share of real incidents that alerted), **detection time**, and **reset time** (how long it keeps firing after the problem ends). A noisy alert has low precision; people learn to ignore it, and then it has low recall too.

The workbook's recommended design for availability alerts is **multiwindow, multi-burn-rate**. Start from a service-level objective (SLO), say 99.9 percent of requests succeed over 30 days. The allowed failures are the **error budget**, and the **burn rate** is how fast you're spending it: burn rate 1 spends exactly the whole budget in the 30 days. For a 99.9 percent SLO the workbook suggests paging when 2 percent of the monthly budget burns within an hour (burn rate 14.4) or 5 percent within six hours (burn rate 6), and opening a ticket when 10 percent burns within three days. Each rule also requires a short window (about one twelfth of the long one) to exceed the same rate, so the alert stops soon after the problem does instead of firing for an hour on stale data.

Translating that to an LLM system:

- **Page** on availability burn (errors, timeouts, and possibly refusals on routes where a refusal is a failure), on latency SLO burn for interactive routes, and on cost burn: spend per hour far above budget usually means a loop, a retry storm, or abuse, and it is both urgent and actionable.
- **Ticket** for quality: a corrected pass rate below threshold over a window, sustained drift, rising thumbs-down rate, a judge whose agreement with fresh human labels dropped.
- **Dashboard only** for everything else.

Two cautions from the workbook apply directly. Low-traffic routes make rates jumpy (one failure in ten requests looks like a disaster), so combine routes, lengthen windows, or add synthetic traffic. And very strict targets can't be defended by alerting alone; at some point the defense is design (fallback models, canary releases) rather than faster pages.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Why the workbook's numbers are what they are">
The thresholds follow from simple arithmetic you can redo for your own SLO. Spending a fraction f of a 30-day (720-hour) budget within a window of w hours requires a burn rate of f times 720 divided by w. For 2 percent in 1 hour that is 0.02 times 720, or 14.4; for 5 percent in 6 hours it is 0.05 times 720 divided by 6, or 6; for 10 percent in 3 days (72 hours) it is 1. Choose f by asking how much of the month's budget you're willing to lose before a person looks, and w by how fast you need to know. For LLM cost alerts, the same structure works with a dollar budget in place of an error budget: the burn rate is spend per hour divided by the budgeted spend per hour, with a long and a short window so a runaway agent loop pages quickly and the page stops once you've killed it.
</GoDeeper>

## What a working team does

Keep one screen per LLM feature, with headline tiles, cost, reliability, and quality, each broken down by model, prompt version, and route. Chart percentiles and rates by failure type, and include refusals and truncations as failures. Run graders on a uniform random sample off the request path, size the sample with the arithmetic above, correct the judge's rate with measured TPR and TNR, and relabel a small sample by hand on a schedule. Watch inputs, outputs, models, and judges for drift against a baseline. Treat feedback as discovery first and a biased measurement second. Page only for symptoms that are urgent and actionable (availability, latency, cost burn), with multiwindow rules; send quality and drift to tickets. Then review the alerts themselves every month: which ones fired, which were real, and which incidents were found by a customer instead. That last list is your alerting system's recall, measured.
</Listen>
