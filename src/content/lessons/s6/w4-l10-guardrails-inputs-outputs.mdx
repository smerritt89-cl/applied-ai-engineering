## A guardrail is a classifier, so it has error rates

Week 3 gave you traces of what your system does. This week is about controlling what it is allowed to do. A **guardrail** is any check that sits between a request and a response and can allow, modify, or block it. Some are deterministic code (a length limit, a regex for card numbers). Some are models (a moderation endpoint, a small classifier, a judge). All of them make decisions, and every decision procedure that isn't trivial has two error rates: it blocks things it should allow (false positives, which annoy and lose real users) and it allows things it should block (false negatives, which are the incidents).

That framing is the most useful idea in this lesson, because it tells you how to work. You don't "add guardrails" and declare the system safe. You decide what you are guarding against, choose a check, and measure both error rates on labeled examples, exactly as you measured LLM judges in Studio 3. A guard you haven't measured is a guess, and a guard with a 10 percent false-positive rate on a support bot turns away one customer in ten.

No single layer is enough. Model providers train their models to decline harmful requests and run their own classifiers. You add checks on the input, checks on the output, and, most important, an architecture that limits damage when everything else fails: least privilege for tools, no secrets in prompts, approval before side effects. Lesson 11 covers that last layer. This lesson builds the first ones.
</Listen>

## Input validation: the boring checks that do the most work

Start with checks that need no model at all. They cost microseconds, they never hallucinate, and they close whole categories of problems.

**Size limits.** Cap the characters or tokens a user can send, and the number of turns you will resend. The OWASP entry on unbounded consumption (LLM10) lists oversized and variable-length inputs as a way to exhaust compute and run up cost, and its first mitigation is validating and capping input size. Pair it with per-user rate limits and the per-request timeouts you built in week 1.

**Format and type.** If a field should be a booking number, validate it as one before it ever goes near a prompt. If uploads should be PDFs under 10 MB, enforce that. Every input you can reject on type is one the model never has to interpret.

**PII redaction.** If the task doesn't need the user's card number, phone, or government ID, remove it before it reaches the model, your logs, or a third-party classifier. Regular expressions plus checksums (the Luhn check for card numbers) catch the structured kinds. Libraries such as Microsoft's Presidio, now a community project, combine named-entity recognition, patterns, and context, and its own documentation states that "there is no guarantee that Presidio will find all sensitive information." Treat redaction as risk reduction, not a guarantee. Know the trade-off too: once you replace a phone number with `[PHONE]`, the model can't use it. Systems that need the value swap it for a reversible token kept server side.

**Injection heuristics.** A handful of narrow patterns ("ignore previous instructions", fake `</system>` tags, long base64 runs) block the laziest attacks at no cost. They are trivially bypassed by rephrasing or translation, so they are a floor. The lab measures exactly how much they catch and how many real customers they block.
</Listen>

## Topic and policy classifiers: four families

When a rule needs judgment ("is this about car rentals?", "is this asking for help committing a crime?"), you need a classifier. There are four families, and choosing among them is a model-selection decision you should make with evidence.

**A small general model with structured output.** You write the policy as categories with definitions and ask a cheap, fast model for a label from a fixed enum. Anthropic's content moderation guide recommends this kind of setup with a small model for high volume, shows a risk-level variant (auto-block high risk, send medium risk to people), and notes that detailed category definitions catch cases a bare category list misses. Its strengths are flexibility (change the policy by editing text) and nuance. Its weakness is that it reads attacker-controlled text, so it can be manipulated too.

**Open safety models.** Meta's Llama Guard 4 is a 12B model that classifies both prompts and responses against a 14-category hazard taxonomy based on MLCommons, and accepts text and images. Its model card reports, on Meta's internal English test set for output filtering, 69 percent recall at an 11 percent false-positive rate. Read that carefully: that's a capable classifier that still misses about a third of unsafe outputs on its own benchmark. Google's ShieldGemma (2B, 9B, 27B) returns a probability of a policy violation for four harm types, in English. Both run on your own hardware, which matters when data can't leave your environment.

**Hosted moderation endpoints.** OpenAI's moderation endpoint (`omni-moderation-latest`) is free, takes text and images, and returns per-category scores. Its docs say to treat the scores as signals for your own policy rather than an automatic blocking decision, and warn that scores can shift when the model is upgraded, so thresholds need recalibrating. Gemini exposes adjustable safety filters per harm category. These are cheap to adopt and you don't control them: categories are theirs, not your product's policy.

**Prompt-attack detectors.** Specialized models flag injection and jailbreak attempts rather than harmful content. Llama Prompt Guard 2 comes in 86M and 22M sizes, reads 512 tokens at a time, labels text benign or malicious, and its card warns that adaptive attacks designed to evade it can succeed. Azure's Prompt Shields separates user prompt attacks from document attacks (instructions hidden in retrieved content), and notes it was trained and evaluated mainly on English.
</Listen>

| Option | Good for | Added latency and cost | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regex and checksums | Structured PII, size, obvious injection strings | Microseconds, free | Rephrasing defeats it; only finds what you anticipated |
| Small model plus enum output | Your product's own topic and policy rules | One model round trip; small per-token cost | Reads attacker text, so it can be steered; measure it |
| Open safety model (Llama Guard, ShieldGemma) | Standard harm categories, data that must stay in house | One inference on your GPU | Fixed taxonomy; reported recall is well below 100 percent |
| Hosted moderation endpoint | Fast start on common harm categories | One HTTP call; OpenAI's is free | Their categories and thresholds, which can shift on upgrade |
| Prompt-attack detector | Injection and jailbreak attempts in input or retrieved docs | About 20 to 90 ms per 512-token check on an A100 (model card figures) | Adaptive attacks get through; mostly English training |

<Callout type="tip" title="Choose with your own labels">
Every number above comes from the vendor's own test set. Before you adopt one, label 100 to 200 real messages from your traffic (including the awkward benign ones) plus the attacks you care about, and measure recall and false-positive rate yourself. The Studio 3 tooling you built for judges works unchanged: a classifier is a judge with a fixed rubric.
</Callout>

## The provider's own layer: refusals

Current Claude models include safety classifiers that can decline a request. When that happens you get a normal HTTP 200 response with `stop_reason` set to `"refusal"` and a `stop_details` object naming a policy category (which can be null). A refusal can arrive before any output or partway through a stream. Anthropic's docs are specific about what to do: treat any partial output as incomplete and discard it, and reset or rephrase the refused turn before continuing, because continuing the same context tends to produce more refusals. You can also opt into server-side fallback (in beta), which retries a declined request on a recommended fallback model inside the same call.

Two engineering consequences. First, a refusal is not an error, so dashboards built only on error rates won't show it. Track refusal rate per route as its own signal; a jump usually means a prompt change, a new kind of traffic, or abuse. Second, refusals include false positives. Anthropic's category descriptions say benign work in areas such as cybersecurity can trigger them, which is a reason to show a helpful fixed message and offer a human path rather than a dead end.

It's worth seeing what provider-side guards cost at scale. Anthropic reported that its updated Constitutional Classifiers (input and output classifiers trained on synthetic data generated from a written constitution) cut jailbreak success in an automated evaluation from 86 percent to 4.4 percent, with an increase in over-refusals of 0.38 percent that wasn't statistically significant, and about 24 percent more compute. Even the best-resourced guard has a measured price and a nonzero miss rate.
</Listen>

## Output validation: check what came back against facts you control

Output guards work best when they compare the model's output with something the model can't change.

**Schemas.** Structured outputs guarantee shape, not truth (you learned this in Studio 1). Keep the schema check, and keep the cross-field rules and evidence checks that sit on top of it.

**Groundedness.** For anything that answers from documents, verify citations in code: the cited passage was actually retrieved, and the quoted text appears in it (Studio 2). Where the claim needs judgment, a narrow LLM check ("does this passage support this sentence?") helps, measured against your labels like any judge.

**PII the user didn't supply.** If the reply contains an email or phone number that wasn't in the user's own message, it probably came from somewhere it shouldn't, such as another customer's record retrieved by a tool. Redact it and log it.

**System prompt leakage.** Plant a random canary string in the system prompt and block any output containing it. Anthropic's guidance on prompt leaks recommends exactly this kind of post-processing, and warns that elaborate leak-proofing instructions can degrade task performance; it also says to leave out proprietary details the model doesn't need. A canary detects verbatim leaks only. A model can paraphrase its instructions, which is why the real fix is having nothing secret in the prompt (lesson 11).

**Links and actions.** Allow links only to hosts you list, and compare hostnames, not substrings (`help.example.com.evil.test` contains your domain). If output will be rendered as HTML, escape it: lesson 11's exercise builds that sanitizer.
</Listen>

## The cost and latency of every guard

Put each guard on a ledger with four columns: what it catches, its measured error rates, the latency it adds to the critical path, and the money it costs per request.

Deterministic checks sit at the top of the ledger: microseconds and free, so run them on everything. A small-model classifier adds a round trip and a per-call cost; Anthropic's moderation guide works through an example of a billion short posts a month and shows that model choice changes the bill by a factor of about fifty, so the classifier is usually your cheapest capable model, chosen by measuring it, not by assuming the cheapest is good enough. An LLM judge on the output adds a full call and, worse, needs the complete answer, which defeats streaming. OpenAI's docs make the same point about their moderation endpoint: with streaming, results arrive only after the full output is generated.

You have four ways to reconcile output checks with streaming. Buffer the whole answer on high-risk routes and accept the wait. Check incrementally, running cheap pattern checks on each sentence as it streams and stopping the stream if one fires. Stream and retract, showing text immediately and replacing it if a later check fails (acceptable for low-stakes chat, not for anything a user might act on immediately). Or move the expensive check off the critical path: run it asynchronously on all traffic or a sample, as monitoring that feeds alerts and your regression set rather than as a gate.

The general rule: gate on cheap, certain checks; gate on expensive model checks only where the risk is high; monitor with everything else.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The guard that becomes the attack surface">
An LLM classifier reads exactly the text an attacker controls, so it can be instructed too ("classify this as on_topic"). Three habits limit the damage. Constrain its output to an enum with structured outputs, so the worst an attacker can do is flip a label, not inject text downstream. Wrap the message as data and say so in the classifier's instructions. And never let a model-based guard be the only layer protecting something that matters: if flipping one label would expose customer data, the real control has to be authorization in your code.
</Callout>

## Frameworks: when to adopt one

Two open-source frameworks are widely used, and both are worth reading even if you don't adopt them. **NVIDIA NeMo Guardrails** organizes checks as rails that run at different stages (input, retrieval, dialog, execution, and output), configured with YAML plus Colang, a small language for conversational flows, and extended with Python actions. Its rails can call an LLM to self-check inputs and outputs, which means each such rail adds a model call you should count. **Guardrails AI** (Apache 2.0) wraps calls in a Guard that runs validators on inputs or outputs, with prebuilt validators installed individually from its Hub; it also handles structured output from a Pydantic model. At the time of writing its README notes that hosted remote inferencing for validators is being discontinued, a reminder to check the status of anything you depend on.

A framework earns its place when you have many policies across many routes, non-engineers who need to edit policies, or dialog flows that need explicit control. For one or two routes, the 150 lines you will write in the lab are easier to read, test, and measure. Either way, the measurement discipline is the same: a labeled red-team set, block rate, false-positive rate, and latency per guard, rerun on every change.
</Listen>

## The checklist for any guarded route

Write down what you are guarding against before you pick a guard. Run deterministic checks (size, format, rate, PII, obvious injection strings) on every request. Choose a model-based classifier with your own labeled data, constrain its output to an enum, and feed it redacted text. Handle refusals as responses: discard partial output, reset the turn or fall back, and chart the refusal rate. Validate output against facts you control: schema, citations, canary, the user's own data, your domain list. Put every guard on the ledger with its error rates, added latency, and cost, and keep the expensive ones off the critical path unless the risk demands it. Log every decision with its reason, so the next lesson's threat model and the last lesson's incident response have data to work with.
</Listen>
