## Delegation is a decision, not a default

After a year of using an agent for everything, the most valuable skill to build is the one that doesn't show up in any feature list: deciding what not to hand over, and how much to hand over when you do. Simon Willison lists "knowing what to outsource" among the practices of engineers who get the most out of coding agents. This lesson gives you a way to make that call deliberately, looks at what agents do poorly, and then turns to a quieter risk: what heavy delegation does to your own skills, and how to keep growing while you use these tools.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## What makes a task risky to delegate

Agents are improving fast, so a list of "things agents can't do" ages badly. Properties of tasks age much better. Five of them predict trouble.

**The outcome can't be checked.** Everything in this elective rests on verification. Where there's no test, no reproducible behavior, no reference output, the agent stops when the work looks done, and so might you. Data analyses with plausible but wrong joins, numerical code without known answers, and UI behavior nobody has specified all fall here until you create the check.

**Mistakes are irreversible or surface late.** Migrations on shared data, deletions, deploys, financial calculations whose errors appear on next month's invoices. Checkpoints don't reach these (lesson 1), and the cost of a miss is high.

**The context is tribal.** When the real requirements live in a spreadsheet, a Slack thread, or a senior engineer's head, the agent fills the gap with a guess. The best-practices doc's first diagnosis for bad sessions is exactly this: information it can't get from the repo.

**The task is long and entangled.** METR's time-horizon research found success falls sharply with task length: models at the time of the study nearly always succeeded on tasks that take experts under about four minutes and rarely on tasks over about four hours, and the length an agent handles at high reliability is much shorter than at 50%. Horizons have grown quickly since, but the shape holds: decompose long tasks into checkable pieces (lesson 6).

**The point is for you to learn.** If you need to own this code, debug it at 2 a.m., or explain it in a design review, delegating the thinking defeats the purpose. That's the subject of the second half of this lesson.

A sixth isn't a property of the task but of the situation: **you can't evaluate the result.** If you couldn't tell a correct solution from a plausible wrong one, you're not delegating, you're hoping. Either learn enough to review it, or bring in someone who can.
</Listen>

<Listen>
## Four modes, not two

The choice isn't "agent or me." There are at least four modes, and most work mixes them.

**Delegate** when the task has a strong check, is reversible, and needs little tribal context: a reproducible bug with a failing test, a dependency upgrade with a good suite, a mechanical migration. Let it run, review the result.

**Delegate with a gate** when the check is good but the stakes are higher: run in plan mode first, require a Stop hook and CI, and keep the irreversible step (merge, deploy, migration) for a person.

**Pair** when the check is weak or the context is tribal: you hold the requirements and the judgment; the agent reads code for you, drafts, explains, and implements in small steps you review as they happen. Turn the weak check into a strong one as you go (write the tests together, from the domain experts' cases).

**Do it yourself** when the goal is learning, when you can't evaluate the result, or when the task is mostly judgment (a product decision, a trade-off between teams, a security design) rather than code. You can still use the agent as a reference or a sparring partner.

The exercise below turns these into an explicit rule. Disagree with its thresholds; the point is to make your rule explicit enough to disagree with.
</Listen>

## What heavy delegation does to skill

In January 2026, Anthropic researchers Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin published a randomized controlled trial on exactly this question. Fifty-two mostly junior software engineers, each with more than a year of weekly Python use, learned Trio, an asynchronous programming library none of them had used. Half had an AI assistant in a sidebar; half coded by hand. Afterwards, everyone took a quiz on the concepts they'd just used.

The AI group averaged 50% on the quiz; the hand-coding group averaged 67%, a difference the authors describe as nearly two letter grades, and statistically significant. The largest gap was on debugging questions. The AI group finished about two minutes faster, but that difference was not statistically significant.

The more useful finding is about how people used the assistant. The authors grouped participants by interaction pattern. The lowest scorers, averaging under 40%, delegated code generation to the AI, increasingly relied on it, or used it to debug iteratively without understanding. The highest scorers, at 65% or more, generated code and then worked to understand it, asked for explanations alongside code, or used the AI only for conceptual questions. The authors' interpretation is that cognitive effort, including getting painfully stuck, is likely important for building mastery.

The limits are real and the authors state them: a small sample, one library, comprehension measured immediately rather than over months, and a chat-style assistant rather than an agentic tool like Claude Code, which they expect could have larger effects. Put it next to the field evidence and a coherent picture emerges. Cui and colleagues found the largest output gains among less experienced developers; this study suggests those same developers are the ones most at risk of not building the skills they'll need to oversee the output. Both can be true, and a team lead should plan for both.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Losing the skills you need for oversight">
The skills that atrophy first under delegation (reading unfamiliar code closely, debugging, knowing why a design is the way it is) are exactly the skills that make you a competent reviewer of agent output. That's a loop worth worrying about: the more you delegate without engaging, the less able you are to catch what goes wrong, and the more you have to trust. Deliberately keep a share of your work in the modes that exercise those skills.
</Callout>

## Habits that keep you learning

These follow from the study's high-scoring patterns and from the verification habits of this elective.

**Predict before you read.** Before opening the agent's diagnosis or fix, write down what you think the cause is. Then compare. You'll learn where your mental model is wrong, and you'll review more carefully.

**Ask why, not just what.** After a change you couldn't have written yourself, ask for an explanation of the key decision and the alternative it rejected, then check the explanation against the code.

**Use learning-oriented modes in areas you want to master.** Claude Code's built-in Explanatory output style adds short "Insight" notes on the choices behind the code; the Learning style also leaves small pieces of real design decisions (marked `TODO(human)`) for you to write, then comments on your code. Switch with `/output-style`. Other tools have similar modes; the study's authors recommend tool designers build more of them.

**Write the first version yourself, sometimes.** For a new library, language, or domain, do the first small task by hand, then compare with what the agent would have done.

**Do the debugging when it's teachable.** When a bug is in a system you'll own, drive the investigation yourself and use the agent as a fast pair of hands (run this, search that), not as the detective.

**Review as practice.** Reading agent code carefully, against a checklist, is itself skill-building. Skimming isn't.
</Listen>

## Your personal operating manual

The elective ends with a document only you can write: how you work with coding agents, written down so you follow it on busy days and revise it as tools change. Keep it to a page. Here's a skeleton to fill in from your lab and project results.

**Defaults.** Tool, model, and effort for everyday work, and when you change each (from lesson 3 and your own comparison).

**Before a task.** Your delegation rule (the exercise above, adjusted). When you write a spec, when you use plan mode, what a good prompt for you includes (the symptom, the place, what done looks like).

**Context.** What lives in your CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md, and your pruning schedule.

**Checks.** Which hooks run in your repos, what your Stop condition is, which paths are protected, what CI enforces.

**Review.** Your checklist and reading order (lesson 8), and when you add a second-model review.

**Security.** What the agent may never touch, which credentials it gets, when you use a container or the sandbox, and how you handle untrusted repositories.

**Learning.** Which skills you're deliberately keeping in your own hands this quarter, and which habits from the list above you're using.

**Measurement.** One number you'll track (cost per merged change, rework minutes, defects found in review) and when you'll look at it.

Date it, keep it in your notes or your dotfiles, and revisit it whenever your tools change in a way that matters. A manual you haven't revised in six months is describing a different set of tools.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Judgment as a calibrated forecast">
A useful way to think about delegation judgment is as calibration, in the forecaster's sense. Before each delegated task, write a one-line prediction: "will succeed without rework," "will need one correction," or "will go wrong." After a few weeks, compare predictions with outcomes. If you're overconfident (predicting success that doesn't come), your rule is delegating too much; if underconfident, you're doing work you could hand off. This is the individual version of what METR did at scale, and it guards against the felt-versus-measured gap from lesson 2. Twenty predictions will teach you more about your own judgment than a year of unexamined use.
</GoDeeper>
