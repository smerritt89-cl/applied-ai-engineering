## The default should be "not yet"

Lesson 1 gave you four questions and lesson 2 gave you five workflow patterns. This lesson makes the case for the conservative answer with numbers, because "agents are less reliable" is easy to say and easy to ignore until you see how fast the arithmetic turns against you.

The Anthropic post that framed this week is explicit that agents bring higher costs and the potential for compounding errors, and recommends extensive testing in sandboxed environments with guardrails. Its broader advice is that many applications never need an agentic system at all, and that optimizing a single call with retrieval and in-context examples is usually enough. Barry Zhang's talk, below, puts the same idea as a working rule: build agents for complex, valuable tasks where errors can be caught, and nowhere else.

None of this is an argument that agents don't work. Coding agents are useful today, and the measurements later in this lesson show capability growing fast. It is an argument about **fit**: for a given task, an agent is one option with a particular cost and failure profile, and you should choose it when the numbers favor it.
</Listen>

## Reliability compounds: the arithmetic

Suppose an agent completes a task in n steps, each step succeeds with probability p, and steps fail independently. Then the whole task succeeds with probability p to the n. That one formula explains most of the gap between demos and production.

Plug in numbers. At p = 0.95, ten steps succeed about 60% of the time and twenty steps about 36%. At p = 0.99, twenty steps succeed about 82%. Inverting the formula, a target success rate T over n steps requires p = T to the power 1/n per step. To finish a 20-step task 90% of the time, each step must succeed about 99.47% of the time. For 99% end to end, each step needs about 99.95%. Taking logs makes the scaling clear: the log of success is n times the log of p, so the failure budget per step shrinks roughly as one over n. Doubling the length of a task roughly halves the per-step error rate you can tolerate.

A workflow is subject to the same arithmetic, which is why lesson 2 put gates between steps. The difference is what you can do about it. In a workflow you know the steps, so you can measure each one, test it, and add a check where it is weak. In an agent the number of steps varies by run, and the model chooses them, so neither n nor the per-step p is under your control. Every additional step the model decides to take is another factor less than one.
</Listen>

## Where the simple model breaks, in both directions

The independent-steps model is a baseline, not a law, and as a mathematician you should ask where it fails. Two corrections matter in practice, and they pull in opposite directions.

**Heterogeneity helps the average.** Real tasks are not all equally hard. If per-step reliability varies across tasks, expected success is the average of p to the n over that distribution, and because x to the n is convex on the unit interval, Jensen's inequality says that average is at least (average p) to the n. Concretely: if half your tasks have p = 1.0 and half have p = 0.9, the mean is 0.95, but 20-step success averages 0.5 times 1 plus 0.5 times 0.12, about 56%, not 36%. The flip side is bimodality: the easy half nearly always succeeds and the hard half nearly always fails, which is a strong argument for routing, so that hard cases go somewhere else.

**Recovery helps, if failures are detectable.** Agents can notice errors and try again. If a step fails with probability 1 minus p, and a failure is detected with probability d and then retried once, the effective per-step success is p plus (1 minus p) times d times p. With p = 0.9 and perfect detection, that is 0.99, a tenfold cut in the per-step failure rate. With d = 0, it is still 0.9. Recovery is only as good as detection, which is the checks-and-balances theme in its sharpest form: tests, validators, and verifiable tool results are what make an agent's retries count. METR's paper reports that the growth in agents' time horizons seems driven largely by greater reliability and ability to adapt to mistakes, along with better reasoning and tool use.

The practical reading: the compounding model is pessimistic for agents with good feedback signals (code with tests), and roughly right, or optimistic, for agents whose errors are silent (a plausible but wrong summary of a contract).
</Listen>

## What a loop costs, and how long it takes

Run the calculator and the loop costs about $2.14 against about $0.064 for the single call, roughly 33 times as much, before a single retry. The structure matters more than the exact numbers. A stateless API means each step resends the whole history, so total input tokens are a sum of a growing sequence: n times the base plus the growth rate times n(n minus 1)/2. Doubling the number of steps roughly quadruples the growth term. Prompt caching changes the price of that resent prefix substantially (on Claude Opus 5.5, cache hits are priced at 5% of base input, per Anthropic's pricing page today), and you should always use it in a loop, but it doesn't change the fact that each step adds context the model must read, and long contexts carry their own quality and limit problems (Studio 1, lesson 1).

Anthropic's multi-agent research post gives production figures in the same direction: in their data, agents used about four times as many tokens as chat interactions, and multi-agent systems about fifteen times. They concluded multi-agent systems need tasks valuable enough to pay for that.

Latency compounds too. Model calls in a loop are sequential by nature: step 5 needs step 4's observation. If each call takes several seconds (more when the model thinks hard), twenty steps take minutes. That rules out agents for anything a user waits on synchronously, and it shapes the product: progress updates, background jobs, and notifications instead of a spinner.

Kapoor and colleagues make the evaluation version of this point in "AI Agents That Matter" (2024): agent benchmarks that report accuracy alone have rewarded needlessly complex and costly systems. They argue for evaluating cost and accuracy jointly, and for comparing against simple baselines. For your team, that means every agent proposal should come with the cost per completed task, not per call, and a comparison with the simplest alternative on the same eval.
</Listen>

## What the measurements say about current agents

Three lines of published evidence are worth knowing, each with its limits.

**Time horizons.** METR (Kwa and colleagues, 2025) proposed measuring agents by the length of tasks, in human expert time, that they complete with 50% success. They timed skilled people on software and research tasks, then fit each model's success rate against task length. At publication, Claude 3.7 Sonnet's 50% horizon was around 50 minutes, and frontier horizons had been doubling roughly every seven months since 2019, possibly faster in 2024. METR's post adds that models of the time succeeded nearly always on tasks under about four minutes and under 10% of the time on tasks over about four hours. METR has published updated measurements since; check their site for current figures rather than relying on these.

Toby Ord (May 2025) noticed that METR's curves are roughly what a **constant hazard rate** would give: a fixed chance of failure per minute of task, so success decays like a half-life, S(t) = 0.5 to the power t over T50. That is the compounding model in continuous time, and it predicts the horizon at success level q is T50 times ln q over ln 0.5: about a third of T50 at 80%, and about 1/70 at 99%. Ord's own 2026 update notes later analysis suggesting hazard rates decline over the course of a task, so treat the half-life as a first approximation. Either way, the lesson for production is the same: the task length an agent handles at the reliability you need is much shorter than its headline horizon.

**Reliability across trials.** tau-bench (Yao and colleagues, 2024), which you met in Studio 3, simulates customer-service conversations and measures pass^k, the chance that all k attempts at a task succeed. At publication, even strong function-calling agents succeeded on fewer than half the tasks, and pass^8 in the retail domain was below 25%. Every user is a new trial, so pass^k is closer to what customers experience than a single-run score.

**Real work.** METR's randomized trial (Becker and colleagues, 2025) had 16 experienced open-source developers complete 246 real tasks in their own repositories, with AI tools allowed or disallowed at random. With early-2025 tools allowed, tasks took 19% longer, while the developers believed AI had sped them up by about 20%. The authors caution against generalizing: a small sample of experts in large, familiar codebases, one period of tools, and METR has since published an update on later tools. The finding worth keeping is methodological: perceived speedups are not measured ones.
</Listen>

## Debugging is the hidden cost

A workflow fails at a step you can name. An agent fails along a trajectory: a sequence of choices, each reasonable-looking, that ends somewhere wrong. Anthropic's multi-agent post describes the problem from production: agents make dynamic decisions and are non-deterministic between runs even with identical prompts, so a user's report that the agent "didn't find obvious information" can't be reproduced by rerunning it. Small changes can cascade into large behavioral changes, and one failed step can send an agent down an entirely different path. Their answer was engineering: full production tracing of agent decisions, the ability to resume from the point of failure rather than restart, and careful rollout of changes so in-flight agents weren't broken.

Count that work in your estimate. An agent you can operate needs trace logging of every model call and tool call, trajectory-level evals with repeated trials (Studio 3, lesson 11), budgets, and someone who reads transcripts. A workflow needs a log line per step and a unit test per branch. If the task doesn't need the agent's flexibility, that operational cost buys nothing.
</Listen>

## The alternatives that usually win

When the four questions say no, or say "not yet", pick from these, simplest first.

**Deterministic code.** If part of the task is parsing, lookup, arithmetic, date resolution, or formatting, do it in code. It is free, instant, and right every time. The best agent designs push everything deterministic out of the model, as the invoice example in the knowledge check below shows.

**A single call with better context.** Many "we need an agent" proposals are really "the model lacks information". Supply it: retrieve the relevant documents (Studio 2), include a few worked examples, put the facts in the prompt, and ask for structured output you can validate. Anthropic's post makes this the default starting point.

**A workflow.** If the task has steps, a chain with gates, a router, or an evaluator-optimizer loop gives you most of the benefit with control you can test. The lab this week builds one.

**An agent with a narrow scope.** If the task really is open-ended, shrink it: read-only tools first, a strict step and cost budget, a human approval on every write, and a workflow around it that handles the common cases. Grow its autonomy as evals and transcripts earn it.

Notice what the list has in common. Each option moves a decision from the model to something you can verify. That is the habit this week is meant to build.
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Reporting cost per call instead of cost per completed task">
Agent costs look small per call. The number that matters is cost per **successfully completed** task: total spend across all steps, retries, and failed runs, divided by the tasks that actually finished correctly. A loop that costs $2 per attempt and succeeds 50% of the time costs $4 per success, before anyone reviews the failures. Compare that number with the simplest alternative on the same eval, and with the cost of a person doing the task.
</Callout>

<GoDeeper title="Making the hazard model precise">
Let the agent's chance of failing in a small interval of task time dt be h dt, with h constant. Then survival S solves S' = minus h S, so S(t) = e to the minus h t, and the 50% horizon is T50 = ln 2 over h. For any success level q, the horizon is T_q = minus ln q over h = T50 times ln q over ln 0.5. That gives T80 at about 0.32 T50 and T99 at about 0.0145 T50. If instead the hazard declines over the task (a Weibull model with shape below 1), long tasks are relatively less hopeless than the exponential predicts, which is the direction of the later analysis Ord cites. A useful exercise: given a model's measured T50 and T80, estimate the Weibull shape parameter k from T80 over T50 equal to (ln 0.8 over ln 0.5) to the power 1/k, and see how sensitive T99 is to k. METR's paper discusses its own curve-fitting choices and robustness checks; the GoDeeper point is that the reliability you need determines which part of the curve you care about, and the tail is where model assumptions matter most.
</GoDeeper>

## Bringing it together

The case against agents by default rests on four facts you can now compute or cite. Success compounds across steps, so long tasks need very high per-step reliability, unless errors are detectable and recoverable. Loops cost more than their per-call price suggests, because context is resent and grows. Trajectories are hard to debug and need real operational investment. And published measurements, read carefully, show capability rising fast on software tasks while consistency and real-world gains remain open questions.

None of that says never. It says: start with code and a single call, add workflow structure where measurement shows a gap, and give the model the loop only for tasks that are complex, valuable, viable, and verifiable. The lab puts this into practice with a status-update pipeline built entirely from workflows, with tests for every branch.
</Listen>
