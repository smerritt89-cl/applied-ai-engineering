## Why RAG exists, and why it fails quietly

The RAG paper (Lewis and colleagues, 2020) framed the problem this studio solves. Language models store a lot of knowledge in their parameters, but they can't easily show where an answer came from or update what they know, and the authors described both as open research problems. Their proposal was a second, **non-parametric** memory: an external index (Wikipedia, in their case) that a retriever reads from and that you can update without retraining. That is what your vector and keyword indexes are.

The catch is that RAG adds stages, and every stage can fail without raising an error. A missed passage doesn't throw an exception. A truncated context doesn't crash. The model writes a fluent answer from whatever it got, and the result looks the same whether the pipeline worked or not. This is why the checks from lesson 10 matter, and why this lesson's main skill is diagnosis: given a wrong answer, find the stage that lost the truth.
</Listen>

## A failure taxonomy you can diagnose

Barnett and colleagues (2024) reported seven failure points from building three RAG systems in research, education, and biomedicine. This course uses six categories that map onto them and onto the stages you built in weeks 1 to 3, because each category points at a different fix.

**Missing content.** The answer isn't in the corpus at all. The correct behavior is to abstain, and the failure is answering anyway (Barnett's FP1). Fix: abstention, and a log of unanswerable questions so the content owners know what to write.

**Retrieval miss.** The answer is in the corpus, but no retriever returned it. Common causes are vocabulary mismatch (the user says "per diem," the policy says "meal limit"), chunks that split the answer, and missing metadata. Fix: hybrid search, query rewriting, better chunking (weeks 2 and 3).

**Ranking miss.** Retrieval found the passage but it ranked below the cutoff (Barnett's FP2). Fix: reranking, a larger k, or both, measured with recall at k.

**Context overflow.** The passage made the cut but was dropped or truncated when the prompt was assembled, because of a token budget or a consolidation step (Barnett's FP3). Fix: budget by tokens, not passage count, and log exactly what reached the model.

**Synthesis error.** The right passage was in the context and the model still got it wrong: didn't extract it, merged two facts, answered too generally or too narrowly, left something out, or ignored the requested format (Barnett's FP4 to FP7). Fix: prompt structure, quote-first prompting, a different model, verification.

**Outdated source.** The answer is faithfully drawn from a passage that is no longer true. Barnett's list doesn't name this one, but it's common in company corpora, and lesson 10's lab showed that every mechanical check passes it. Fix: at ingestion (remove or flag superseded documents, store effective dates), and in the prompt (prefer the newest).
</Listen>

| Category | Where the truth was lost | Barnett et al. (2024) | First fix to try |
|---|---|---|---|
| Missing content | Never in the corpus | FP1 Missing content | Abstain; log the gap |
| Retrieval miss | Not in the candidates | (retrieval stage, before FP2) | Hybrid search, query rewriting, chunking |
| Ranking miss | In candidates, below the cutoff | FP2 Missed the top ranked documents | Rerank, tune k |
| Context overflow | Selected, then dropped from the prompt | FP3 Not in context | Token budgeting, log the final context |

| Outdated source | Faithfully quoted, no longer true | (not in their list) | Ingestion rules, dates as metadata |

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Tuning the prompt when retrieval is broken">
The most common wasted week in RAG work: answers are wrong, so someone iterates on the generation prompt. If the right passage never reached the model, no prompt can fix it, and every "improvement" is noise you are fitting. Check retrieval first. On your eval set, measure recall at k for the gold passages before you look at a single generated answer. If recall is 70 percent, at least 30 percent of your wrong answers are not the generator's fault.
</Callout>

## Reading a trace

Diagnosis needs data, and the data has to be logged at the time, because you can't reconstruct what the retriever returned last Tuesday. For each question, a trace should record: the query as received and as rewritten; the ranked candidates from each retriever with scores; what the reranker kept; the exact passage ids (and token counts) that went into the prompt; the answer, its citations, and the verifier's results; and whether it abstained.

With a labeled question (one where you know the gold passage ids), the diagnosis is a walk down the pipeline. Is there a gold passage at all? If not, the only correct outcome was abstention. Did the answer cite an outdated passage? Was the gold passage among the candidates? Did it rank above the cutoff? Did it reach the prompt? If it reached the prompt and the answer is still wrong, it's synthesis. The first stage that lost the gold passage owns the failure. You'll implement exactly this walk in the exercise, and in Studio 3 you'll run it over hundreds of logged questions to get a failure histogram, which tells you where your next week of work should go.

Barnett and colleagues draw a lesson from their case studies that applies directly: a RAG system can only be fully validated while it runs, because realistic questions arrive only once real users do, and robustness is something you build up over time rather than design in up front. That's an argument for logging traces from the first day.
</Listen>

## Abstaining on purpose

"I don't know based on the provided sources" is a correct answer whenever the sources don't contain one. Anthropic's first recommendation for reducing hallucinations is to give the model explicit permission to admit uncertainty, and in RAG you can go further, because you have signals the model doesn't.

Design abstention in three layers. **In the prompt**, say exactly when to abstain and give one exact sentence, so code can detect it without guessing. **Before generation**, add a cheap gate: if the best retrieval or reranker score is below a threshold you calibrated on your eval set, skip the model call and abstain, or ask a clarifying question. That saves money on questions you can't answer and removes the temptation for the model to improvise. **After generation**, treat an answer whose substantive claims have no verified citations as an abstention in disguise: show the sources you found and say they don't settle it, rather than shipping unsupported text.

Then measure it, in two directions, because each can be gamed alone. **Abstention recall** is the share of unanswerable questions where the system abstained. **False abstention rate** is the share of answerable questions where it abstained anyway. A system that always says "I don't know" has perfect recall and is useless. Your eval set needs both kinds of question for these numbers to exist, which is one of the requirements in lesson 12.
</Listen>

## Query routing: not every question should hit the index

A RAG system usually receives several kinds of input: questions the corpus answers, questions it can't (out of scope), small talk, requests that need structured data (a count, a date range, something a database or API answers better than text search), and questions that need several lookups. Sending all of them through the same retrieve-then-generate path wastes money on some and gives poor answers to others.

A **router** is a classification step before retrieval: given the query, pick a path. It can be rules (a regular expression for order numbers), a small classifier, or a call to a small, fast model with a short list of labels and structured output. This is the classic place for the cheapest tier, such as `claude-haiku-5-5`: the task is narrow, the output is a label, and you can measure its accuracy directly on labeled queries. A router you haven't measured just moves errors earlier in the pipeline, where they're harder to see.

Routing can also choose *how* to answer. Li and colleagues (2024) compared RAG with long-context models across several public benchmarks and found that, given enough resources, long context did better on average while RAG cost far less. Their Self-Route method lets the model judge whether the retrieved chunks are enough to answer; if not, the query is escalated to the long-context path. They report much lower compute cost than always using long context with comparable performance. You don't have to copy it to use the idea: try the cheap path first, check a signal you trust, and escalate only when the signal says so.
</Listen>

## Long context, caching, or RAG?

Current frontier models accept very long inputs (Claude Opus 5.5 has a one-million-token context window), so a fair question for any corpus is: why retrieve at all? Anthropic's own contextual retrieval write-up (September 2024) gives a simple rule of thumb: if the knowledge base is under about 200,000 tokens (around 500 pages), you can include all of it in the prompt, and prompt caching makes that much cheaper and faster. Compare the options on four axes.

**Cost.** Without caching, you pay for the whole corpus on every question. With caching, the corpus is a stable prefix: you pay a cache write when it's created (1.25 times the input price for the five-minute lifetime) and a much lower read price afterwards (on Claude Opus 5.5, 0.05 times the input price at the time of writing; 0.1 times on many other models). Each hit refreshes the lifetime for free, so steady traffic keeps the cache warm.

**Latency.** Processing a long prompt takes time even when it's cheap. Caching helps a lot here (Anthropic reported more than a twofold latency reduction in that write-up), but a RAG prompt of a few thousand tokens is still shorter.

**Accuracy.** Long context removes retrieval misses, ranking misses, and context overflow in one stroke, which is why it often wins on average in studies like Li and colleagues'. It doesn't remove synthesis errors, and "lost in the middle" effects mean the model can still overlook a passage it was given. Barnett and colleagues also noted that larger context windows improved answer accuracy in their systems, which is consistent with this.

**Freshness and scale.** Every change to the corpus invalidates the cached prefix after the point of change. Corpora that are large, change often, or need per-user permissions (each user sees different documents) push you toward retrieval.

The conclusion is not "RAG is obsolete" or "long context is a gimmick." It's that for a small, stable corpus, long context with caching is the baseline your RAG system must beat on your eval set, and for a large or fast-changing one, RAG is the only option that scales.
</Listen>

```python
# Back-of-envelope: one question, input side only (output tokens cost the same either way).
# Claude Opus 5.5 prices from the pricing page on 2026-11-11, dollars per million tokens.
INPUT, CACHE_WRITE_5M, CACHE_READ = 4.00, 5.00, 0.20

corpus = 150_000          # tokens of documentation
rag_prompt = 9_000        # 20 retrieved chunks of about 400 tokens, plus instructions and question

full_context = corpus * INPUT / 1e6           # $0.60 per question, every question
cached_read = corpus * CACHE_READ / 1e6       # $0.03 per question once the cache is warm
cache_write = corpus * CACHE_WRITE_5M / 1e6   # $0.75 each time the prefix is (re)written
rag = rag_prompt * INPUT / 1e6                # $0.036 per question

print(full_context, cached_read, cache_write, rag)
```

## What the numbers say

With these assumptions, a warm cache makes the whole-corpus prompt about as cheap per question as a RAG prompt sixteen times shorter: 3 cents against 3.6 cents of input. Uncached, it is more than sixteen times the price. Two conditions make the cached number real. Traffic must be steady enough that the five-minute cache rarely expires (otherwise you pay 75 cents in writes again and again, or you pay for the one-hour lifetime at twice the input price per write), and the corpus must stay the same between writes.

Now change one assumption: a 2-million-token corpus. It no longer fits in the context window, and even a cached read would cost 40 cents per question on this model. Retrieval is back. Or switch the generator to `claude-haiku-5-5`: the input price is far lower, but prompts over 100,000 tokens are billed at a higher rate on that model, which is the kind of detail that makes "check the pricing page" a real instruction rather than a disclaimer. Run the numbers for your corpus, then run the eval, because the cheapest configuration that fails your accuracy bar is not cheap.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Lessons from the seven failure points paper">
Barnett and colleagues' paper is an experience report, not a benchmark, and it's worth reading as one. Their case studies were a tool that helps researchers analyze and rank scientific papers, an AI tutor answering students from course content (piloted with about 200 students), and a larger test on the BioASQ biomedical dataset with roughly 4,000 documents and 1,000 questions. Beyond the failure points, they list practical findings: metadata such as file names and chunk numbers improved retrieval, semantic caching cut cost and latency, and chunking, retrieval, and prompts all needed calibration against real questions. Their two headline lessons (validate during operation; robustness evolves) are the case for this studio's project requirements: an eval set from the start, and logs designed for the evaluation work in Studio 3. For the practice of building evals from real traces, Hamel Husain's "Your AI Product Needs Evals" is a good companion.
</GoDeeper>

## What to carry forward

When an answer is wrong, find the stage that lost the truth before touching anything. Log traces that make that possible: ranks, cutoffs, final context, citations, verifier results. Make abstention a designed path with an exact phrase, a score gate, and two numbers you track. Route before you retrieve, with the cheapest model that measurably does the job. And for any corpus small enough to fit, test long context with caching as a baseline, because it deletes three failure categories at once. The last lesson puts these decisions into one design.
</Listen>
