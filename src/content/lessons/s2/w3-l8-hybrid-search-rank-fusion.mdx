## Two retrievers, two lists, one answer

Last lesson ended with a finding that keeps recurring in the literature and in practice: keyword search and embedding search fail on different queries. BM25 nails "error TS-999" and misses "how do I get my money back". Embeddings do the reverse. **Hybrid search** runs both and combines the results.

The architecture is simple. Each retriever returns its own top N candidates for the query, typically a few dozen to a hundred. A **fusion** step merges the lists into one ranking. The top k of that ranking goes on to a reranker (lesson 9) or straight into the prompt. Anthropic's contextual retrieval post describes exactly this pipeline: retrieve by embeddings, retrieve by BM25, then combine and deduplicate using rank fusion. In their tests across several knowledge domains, embeddings plus BM25 retrieved better than embeddings alone.

The interesting question is the fusion step. There are two families. **Score fusion** combines the retrievers' scores, usually a weighted sum. **Rank fusion** ignores scores and combines positions. Each has a real argument behind it, and the choice matters more than it looks.
</Listen>

## Why you can't just add the scores

The tempting first version is `alpha * vector_score + (1 - alpha) * bm25_score`. With raw scores this is almost always wrong, because the two numbers live on different scales.

A BM25 score is a sum of positive terms, one per matching query word, each bounded by roughly (k1 + 1) times that word's idf. So it grows with the length of the query and the rarity of its words, and it changes when the corpus changes, because idf does. Scores of 5 or 25 are both ordinary. A cosine similarity is bounded between minus 1 and 1, and for many embedding models the scores of plausible candidates sit in a narrow band. Add a number that varies by 10 to a number that varies by 0.05, and the first decides the ranking. Your alpha of 0.5 is not the weight you think it is.

The standard repair is to **normalize per query**, most often with min-max scaling: within each retriever's result list, map the best score to 1 and the worst to 0. Weaviate's `relativeScoreFusion`, its default since version 1.24, does this and then takes a weighted sum. Its documentation makes a fair case for score fusion: scores carry information that ranks throw away. If one keyword result is far ahead of the rest, a score-based method can keep that gap; a rank-based method sees only "first".

Min-max has its own failure modes, and you should know them before trusting it. The best result in each list always becomes 1, even when every result is poor, so a retriever that found nothing relevant still contributes full-strength votes. The worst result always becomes 0, however good it was. A single outlier squeezes everyone else toward 0. And with one result, or tied results, the formula divides by zero, so you need a rule for that case.
</Listen>

## Reciprocal rank fusion

Cormack, Clarke, and Buettcher introduced **reciprocal rank fusion** at SIGIR 2009, almost as an afterthought: they wanted a simple unsupervised baseline while studying learning-to-rank methods, and found the baseline hard to beat. The formula: for each document d, sum over the input rankings r of 1 / (k + r(d)), where r(d) is d's position (1 for the top) and a document absent from a list contributes nothing from that list. Sort by the sum.

The constant k does real work. Without it (k = 0), being first in one list is worth 1 and being second is worth 0.5, so one system's top pick dominates. With k = 60, first is worth 1/61 and second 1/62: nearly the same. The authors say k "mitigates the impact of high rankings by outlier systems". They fixed k = 60 in a pilot study, reported it as near-optimal but not critical, and never changed it. Across their TREC experiments, RRF beat Condorcet Fuse and CombMNZ (two established fusion methods) and usually beat the best individual system, by 4 to 5 percent on average. Elasticsearch's RRF retriever uses the same formula, with `rank_constant` defaulting to 60 and a `rank_window_size` that sets how many results each retriever contributes.

What RRF buys you is that it needs no calibration. Ranks are comparable across any retrievers, so there is nothing to normalize and nothing to tune at first. What it costs you is the information in the scores: a runaway winner and a near tie look the same. It also weighs every retriever equally unless your engine offers per-retriever weights, and documents found by only one retriever are at a disadvantage, which is usually what you want (agreement is evidence) but not always (an exact code match found only by BM25 may be the single right answer).
</Listen>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Tuning fusion on the queries you happen to remember">
It is easy to tweak alpha or k until the three queries in your head look right, and make the other three hundred worse. Fusion parameters are hyperparameters. Change them only against a labeled query set, report recall and MRR before and after, and keep a slice of queries you didn't look at while tuning, for the same reason you'd hold out a test set in any fitting problem.
</Callout>

## Fixing the query instead of the ranking

Sometimes the problem isn't how you combine results but what you searched for. Three techniques rewrite or expand the query before retrieval.

**Rewriting conversational queries.** In a chat, the user's third message might be "what about for contractors?" On its own, that retrieves nothing useful. A model call that rewrites it into a standalone query, using the conversation ("What is the expense reimbursement policy for contractors?"), is a common, cheap fix in chat-based RAG. Check it the way you check any model output: log the rewritten query next to the original so you can see when the rewrite drifts, and make sure it never invents constraints the user didn't state.

**Multi-query expansion.** Generate a few paraphrases of the query, retrieve for each, and fuse the lists with RRF. It raises recall for vague questions, at the cost of several retrievals per query. For keyword search specifically, a hand-maintained synonym list for your domain ("PTO", "vacation", "annual leave") is cheaper and fully predictable.

**HyDE.** Gao, Ma, Lin, and Callan (2022) proposed Hypothetical Document Embeddings. A query and the passage that answers it often look quite different: a short question versus a paragraph of explanation. HyDE asks an instruction-following model to write a hypothetical document that would answer the query, embeds that document, and searches the real corpus with its embedding. The paper is candid that the generated document may contain false details; the claim is that the search over real documents grounds the result, because the embedding captures relevance patterns rather than specific facts. In their experiments HyDE beat the unsupervised dense retriever Contriever and was competitive with fine-tuned retrievers, across web search, QA, and fact verification tasks and several languages.

Read the setting carefully: HyDE was shown for **zero-shot** dense retrieval, with no relevance labels. With a strong modern embedding model or a hybrid pipeline, the gain may be smaller, and every query now pays for a generation call. The hypothetical document is a search key, never something to show a user or cite. As always, measure it on your queries before keeping it.
</Listen>

## Metadata filters, and the order of operations

Real queries carry constraints that no embedding can express: only documents from this product line, only policies effective this year, only content this user is allowed to see. Qdrant's documentation states the point plainly: filters exist because you can't put every property of an object into a vector. Vector databases and search engines let you attach structured fields (often called payload or metadata) and filter on them, and they recommend indexing the fields you filter on.

Where the filter runs matters. **Post-filtering** retrieves the top k by similarity and then drops the ones that fail the filter. If the filter is selective, you may be left with two results out of ten, or none, even though plenty of matching documents exist further down. **Filtering inside the search** (restricting the candidates as the index is searched) avoids that, but approximate nearest neighbor indexes can lose recall when a filter removes most of the graph. Qdrant's 2019 engineering article on filterable HNSW describes this failure and its fixes; modern engines handle it in various ways, so read your engine's docs on filtered search and test recall with your most selective filters.

In hybrid search, apply the same filters to **both** retrievers before fusion. If only the vector side is filtered, the BM25 side can bring back documents the filter was meant to exclude, and fusion will happily rank them.

That leads to the most important rule in this section: **permissions are filters, enforced in code.** Never retrieve everything and ask the model to ignore documents the user may not see. The model reads whatever is in its context, and anything in its context can end up in its answer. OWASP's Top 10 for LLM applications lists sensitive information disclosure among its main risks, and retrieval without access control is a direct path to it. Test this explicitly: a user without access runs a query whose best answer is in a restricted document, and the test asserts that document never appears in the retrieved set.
</Listen>

<GoDeeper title="Why 1 / (k + r) and not something else?">
Cormack and colleagues chose a reciprocal because high ranks should matter more than low ones, but low ranks shouldn't vanish entirely, as they would with an exponential decay. You can view RRF as a smoothed Borda count. Borda gives each position a linear number of points; RRF gives 1 / (k + r), which for large k is approximately 1/k minus r/k², nearly linear in r over the top few dozen ranks. As k shrinks, the function becomes steeply convex and rewards top positions much more. That interpolation is why the paper found k "not critical": across a broad middle range, the induced ordering barely changes. A useful exercise is to fuse the lists in the PyRunner above with k from 1 to 1000 and see where the order flips.
</GoDeeper>

## How a working team would decide

Start with RRF, because it needs no calibration and is a strong default with a peer-reviewed track record. Apply filters, including permissions, identically in both retrievers. Build the labeled query set from lesson 9, and measure BM25 alone, vectors alone, and the fusion. Then try normalized score fusion and one query technique at a time (rewriting for chat, HyDE or multi-query for vague questions), keeping each only if it beats the baseline on held-out queries by more than the noise between runs. Write the result down with the numbers; the next person to touch the pipeline will want to know why it is built the way it is. This week's lab is exactly that experiment, run offline.
</Listen>
